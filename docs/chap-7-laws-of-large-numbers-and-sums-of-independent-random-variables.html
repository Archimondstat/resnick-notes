Notes on A Probability Path (Resnick)

Chap 7, Laws of Large Numbers and Sums of Independent Random Variables

Summary

7.1–7.2

From this chapter on, we start to study the asymptotic behaviour of random sums and sample means, such as

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n X_i, \qquad \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i. \]

Unlike the previous chapters, for arbitrary iid random variables \(\{X_n\}\), we may not have finite first or second moments. Therefore, instead of attacking the original sequence directly, we introduce a truncated sequence

\[ X_n \mathbf1_{\{|X_n|\le n\}}. \]

The truncation level is chosen carefully so that the probability of truncation can be controlled.

This naturally introduces the idea of tail equivalence. Even though two random variable sequences may be different,

\[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty P(X_n\neq X_n')<\infty, \]

implies, by the Borel–Cantelli lemma, that they differ only finitely many times with probability one. In other words, they are eventually identical almost surely and therefore share the same asymptotic behaviour.

Proposition 7.1.1 provides the theoretical foundation for this idea. It allows us to first study the sum or sample mean of a truncated random variable sequence, which is usually easier to handle, and then transfer the result back to the original sequence.

In fact, one of the main ideas of this chapter is not to fight heavy tails directly, but to replace a difficult sequence by an asymptotically equivalent and more manageable one.

Section 7.2 develops the Generalized Weak Law of Large Numbers. The truncated variables possess the moment properties needed for the ordinary WLLN, even when the original random variables may not have finite mean or variance.

The proof strategy is actually very simple:

  1. Construct a truncated sequence.
  2. Apply the ordinary WLLN to the truncated sample mean.
  3. Show that the original and truncated sample means are tail equivalent.
  4. Transfer the convergence result back to the original sample mean.

7.3

We started from Proposition 7.3.1, which is the Skorohod’s inequality.

It gave us a method to bound

\[ P\left(\sup_{j\le n}|S_j|>2\lambda\right) \]

without requiring the assumption of the existence of any moment.

Using this inequality, Lévy’s theorem (Theorem 7.3.2) gave us a surprising result, which is that for

\[ S_n=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i, \]

where \(\{X_n\}\) is independent,

\[ S_n \xrightarrow{P} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S_n \xrightarrow{a.s.}. \]

Recall that for a general sequence of random variables, convergence in probability only guarantees the existence of an almost surely convergent subsequence.

In the end, the Kolmogorov Convergence Criterion (Theorem 7.3.3) told us that for independent random variables \(\{X_n\}\), if

\[ \sum \operatorname{Var}(X_n)<\infty, \]

then

\[ \sum (X_n-E(X_n)) \]

converges almost surely, as a result of Lévy’s theorem.

In summary, this section gave us several tools to investigate the convergence properties of partial sums of independent random variables. Starting from an inequality for controlling the maximum partial sum, we obtained a surprising equivalence between convergence in probability and almost sure convergence for partial sums, and finally applied it to establish the Kolmogorov Convergence Criterion.

7.4

Before 7.4, we mainly discussed the convergence problem in the form

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} X_i \text{ converges almost surely.} \]

In 7.4, we introduced Kronecker’s lemma to allow us to discuss

\[ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i. \]

Kronecker’s lemma acts as a bridge from the convergence of an infinite series to the convergence of its corresponding averages. It therefore allows us to transfer results about the convergence of series into results about the convergence of sample averages.

In summary, this section built the connection between the convergence of series and the convergence of sample means, which prepares us for the Strong Law of Large Numbers in the following sections.

Exercises

Ex 2

Let \(\{X_n\}\) be iid, \(EX_n=\mu\), \(\operatorname{Var}(X_n)=\sigma^2\). Set

\[ \bar X=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n X_i}{n}. \]

Show that

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)^2 \xrightarrow{P} \sigma^2. \]

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

We first use the identity

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)^2 = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2-\bar X^2. \]

Since

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X_1)=\sigma^2<\infty, \]

we have

\[ E(X_1^2)<\infty. \]

Also, since \(\{X_i\}\) are iid, \(\{X_i^2\}\) are iid. Therefore, by Khintchin’s WLLN,

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2 \xrightarrow{P} E(X_1^2). \]

Again by Khintchin’s WLLN,

\[ \bar X = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \xrightarrow{P} E(X_1)=\mu. \]

Since \(f(x)=x^2\) is continuous, we have

\[ \bar X^2 \xrightarrow{P} \mu^2. \]

Hence

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)^2 = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2-\bar X^2 \xrightarrow{P} E(X_1^2)-\mu^2. \]

But

\[ E(X_1^2)-\mu^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X_1) = \sigma^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)^2 \xrightarrow{P} \sigma^2. \]

Ex 3

Occupancy problems. Randomly distribute \(r\) balls in \(n\) boxes so that the sample space \(\Omega\) consists of \(n^r\) equally likely elements. Write

\[ N_n=\sum_{i=1}^n 1_{[\text{\(i\)th box is empty}]} \]

for the number of empty boxes. Check

\[ P[\text{\(i\)th box is empty}] = \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r \]

so that

\[ E(N_n)=n(1-n^{-1})^r. \]

Check that as \(r/n\to c\)

\[ E(N_n)/n\to e^{-c} \]

\[ N_n/n\xrightarrow{P}e^{-c}. \]

For the second result, compute \(\operatorname{Var}(N_n)\) and show \(\operatorname{Var}(N_n/n)\to0\).

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Let

\[ I_i=1_{[\text{\(i\)th box is empty}]}. \]

Then

\[ N_n=\sum_{i=1}^n I_i. \]

For a fixed box \(i\), one ball avoids box \(i\) with probability

\[ 1-\frac1n. \]

Since there are \(r\) balls,

\[ P[I_i=1] = P[\text{\(i\)th box is empty}] = \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r. \]

Therefore,

\[ E(N_n) = E\left(\sum_{i=1}^n I_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n E(I_i) = n\left(1-\frac1n\right)^r. \]

Hence

\[ \frac{E(N_n)}{n} = \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r = \left[\left(1-\frac1n\right)^n\right]^{r/n} \to e^{-c}, \]

since \(r/n\to c\).

Now we compute the variance. Since \(I_i^2=I_i\),

\[ \operatorname{Var}(I_i) = E(I_i^2)-[E(I_i)]^2 = E(I_i)-[E(I_i)]^2. \]

Thus

\[ \operatorname{Var}(I_i) = \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r}. \]

For \(i\ne j\),

\[ E(I_iI_j) = P[I_i=1,I_j=1]. \]

The event \(\{I_i=1,I_j=1\}\) means that both the \(i\)th and \(j\)th boxes are empty. For one ball to avoid both boxes, the probability is

\[ 1-\frac2n. \]

Therefore,

\[ E(I_iI_j) = \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r. \]

So

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(I_i,I_j) = E(I_iI_j)-E(I_i)E(I_j) = \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \operatorname{Var}(N_n) = \operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^n I_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n \operatorname{Var}(I_i) + \sum_{i\ne j}\operatorname{Cov}(I_i,I_j). \]

Hence

\[ \operatorname{Var}(N_n) = n\left[ \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} \right] + n(n-1) \left[ \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} \right]. \]

Dividing by \(n^2\),

\[ \operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{N_n}{n}\right) = \frac1n \left[ \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} \right] + \frac{n-1}{n} \left[ \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} \right]. \]

For the first term,

\[ 0 \le \frac1n \left[ \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} \right] \le \frac1n \to0. \]

For the second term, since \(r/n\to c\),

\[ \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r \to e^{-2c}, \]

and

\[ \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} = \left[\left(1-\frac1n\right)^n\right]^{2r/n} \to e^{-2c}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} \to0. \]

Thus

\[ \operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{N_n}{n}\right)\to0. \]

By Chebyshev’s inequality, for every \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[ P\left[ \left| \frac{N_n}{n} - \frac{E(N_n)}{n} \right| > \varepsilon \right] \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(N_n/n)}{\varepsilon^2} \to0. \]

So

\[ \frac{N_n}{n} - \frac{E(N_n)}{n} \xrightarrow{P}0. \]

Since

\[ \frac{E(N_n)}{n}\to e^{-c}, \]

we conclude that

\[ \frac{N_n}{n} \xrightarrow{P} e^{-c}. \]

\(\textbf{Note:}\) In this exercise, we cannot apply Khintchin’s WLLN directly to

\[ \frac{N_n}{n} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n I_i, \qquad I_i=1_{[\text{\(i\)th box is empty}]}. \]

Although the \(I_i\)’s have the same distribution, they are not independent. Indeed, for \(i\ne j\),

\[ P(I_i=1,I_j=1) = \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r \ne \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} = P(I_i=1)P(I_j=1). \]

Therefore, instead of using Khintchin’s WLLN directly, we follow the hint: compute \(\operatorname{Var}(N_n)\), show

\[ \operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{N_n}{n}\right)\to0, \]

and then use Chebyshev’s inequality.

Ex 4

Suppose \(g:[0,1]\to \mathbb{R}\) is measurable and Lebesgue integrable. Let \(\{U_n,n\ge 1\}\) be iid uniform random variables and define \(X_i=g(U_i)\). In what sense does \(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i/n\) approximate \(\int_0^1 g(x)dx\)? (This offers a way to approximate the integral by Monte Carlo methods.) How would one guarantee a desired degree of precision?

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

\(\textbf{Pre-learning:}\) By Kolmogorov’s SLLN, if \(\{X_n,n\ge1\}\) are iid and

\[ E|X_1|<\infty, \]

then

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \xrightarrow{a.s.} E(X_1). \]

Now let

\[ X_i=g(U_i), \]

where \(\{U_i,i\ge1\}\) are iid uniform random variables on \([0,1]\).

Since \(g\) is measurable, \(X_i=g(U_i)\) is a random variable. Since the \(U_i\)’s are iid, the \(X_i\)’s are also iid.

Also, since \(g\) is Lebesgue integrable,

\[ E|X_i| = E|g(U_i)| = \int_0^1 |g(x)|\,dx <\infty. \]

Moreover,

\[ E(X_i) = E[g(U_i)] = \int_0^1 g(x)\,dx. \]

Therefore, by Kolmogorov’s SLLN,

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n g(U_i) \xrightarrow{a.s.} E(X_1) = \int_0^1 g(x)\,dx. \]

Thus \(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i/n\) approximates \(\int_0^1 g(x)dx\) in the almost sure sense. Since almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability, we also have

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \xrightarrow{P} \int_0^1 g(x)\,dx. \]

To guarantee a desired degree of precision, we can state the guarantee in an \((\varepsilon,\delta)\) sense. For any \(\varepsilon>0\) and \(\delta>0\), since

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \xrightarrow{P} \int_0^1 g(x)\,dx, \]

there exists \(N>0\) such that for all \(n>N\),

\[ P\left[ \left| \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i - \int_0^1 g(x)\,dx \right| > \varepsilon \right] < \delta. \]

So, by choosing \(n\) sufficiently large, the Monte Carlo approximation can be made accurate within error \(\varepsilon\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\).

\(\textbf{Note:}\) In this exercise, I used a theorem not yet covered in our reading order, namely Theorem 7.5.1. I treat this as a pre-learning step.

Further, the SLLN gives the stronger asymptotic statement, namely almost sure convergence. The \((\varepsilon,\delta)\) statement is the practical probability guarantee. Under only \(g\in L^1[0,1]\), this guarantees the existence of a sufficiently large \(n\), but it does not give an explicit computable formula for \(n\). For an explicit Chebyshev-type bound, one would need a stronger assumption such as

\[ E[g(U)^2]<\infty. \]

Ex 21

Suppose \(\{X_n(\theta), n\ge 1\}\) are iid with common exponential distribution with mean \(\theta\). Then

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n X_i(\theta)/n \xrightarrow{P} \theta. \]

Show for any \(u:\mathbb{R}_+\mapsto \mathbb{R}_+\) which is bounded and continuous that

\[ \int_0^\infty u(y)e^{-ny\theta^{-1}} \frac{(ny\theta^{-1})^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} n\theta^{-1}\,dy \to u(\theta). \]

Show this convergence is uniform on finite \(\theta\)-intervals. (Compare this with the Bernstein polynomials of item 6.3 on page 176.)

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Let

\[ S_n=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i(\theta), \qquad Y_n=\frac{S_n}{n}. \]

Since \(X_i(\theta)\) are iid exponential random variables with mean \(\theta\), we have

\[ S_n\sim \operatorname{Gamma}(n,\theta), \]

with density

\[ f_{S_n}(x) = e^{-x\theta^{-1}} \frac{x^{n-1}\theta^{-n}}{(n-1)!}, \qquad x>0. \]

Now

\[ P(Y_n\le y) = P(S_n\le ny). \]

Therefore the density of \(Y_n\) is

\[ f_{Y_n}(y) = n f_{S_n}(ny). \]

Hence

\[ f_{Y_n}(y) = n e^{-ny\theta^{-1}} \frac{(ny)^{n-1}\theta^{-n}}{(n-1)!}. \]

Since

\[ n(ny)^{n-1}\theta^{-n} = n\theta^{-1}(ny\theta^{-1})^{n-1}, \]

we get

\[ f_{Y_n}(y) = e^{-ny\theta^{-1}} \frac{(ny\theta^{-1})^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} n\theta^{-1}, \qquad y>0. \]

Thus the integral in the exercise is

\[ \int_0^\infty u(y)e^{-ny\theta^{-1}} \frac{(ny\theta^{-1})^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} n\theta^{-1}\,dy = E_\theta[u(Y_n)]. \]

So it is enough to prove

\[ E_\theta[u(Y_n)]\to u(\theta). \]

Since

\[ Y_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i(\theta), \]

and

\[ E_\theta[X_1(\theta)]=\theta, \]

we have

\[ Y_n\xrightarrow{P_\theta}\theta. \]

Fix \(\varepsilon>0\). Since \(u\) is continuous at \(\theta\), there exists \(\delta>0\) such that

\[ |y-\theta|<\delta \]

implies

\[ |u(y)-u(\theta)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)| &= |E_\theta[u(Y_n)-u(\theta)]| \\ &\le E_\theta|u(Y_n)-u(\theta)| \\ &= E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|<\delta\}} \right] \\ &\quad+ E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|\ge \delta\}} \right]. \end{aligned} \]

For the first term,

\[ E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|<\delta\}} \right] \le \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

For the second term, since \(u\) is bounded,

\[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)| \le 2\|u\|_\infty. \]

Therefore

\[ E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|\ge \delta\}} \right] \le 2\|u\|_\infty P_\theta(|Y_n-\theta|\ge \delta). \]

Since

\[ Y_n\xrightarrow{P_\theta}\theta, \]

we have

\[ P_\theta(|Y_n-\theta|\ge \delta)\to0. \]

Thus, for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ 2\|u\|_\infty P_\theta(|Y_n-\theta|\ge \delta) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Hence

\[ |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)|<\varepsilon \]

for sufficiently large \(n\). Therefore

\[ E_\theta[u(Y_n)]\to u(\theta). \]

Equivalently,

\[ \int_0^\infty u(y)e^{-ny\theta^{-1}} \frac{(ny\theta^{-1})^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} n\theta^{-1}\,dy \to u(\theta). \]

Now we prove the convergence is uniform on finite \(\theta\)-intervals. Fix \(T<\infty\). We want to show

\[ \sup_{0<\theta\le T} |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)| \to0. \]

Let \(\eta>0\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)| &\le E_\theta|u(Y_n)-u(\theta)| \\ &= E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|<\eta\}} \right] \\ &\quad+ E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|\ge\eta\}} \right]. \end{aligned} \]

For the first term, if

\[ 0<\theta\le T \]

and

\[ |Y_n-\theta|<\eta, \]

then

\[ Y_n\in[0,T+\eta]. \]

Since \(u\) is continuous, it is uniformly continuous on the compact interval \([0,T+\eta]\). Define

\[ \omega_T(\eta) = \sup_{\substack{x,y\in[0,T+\eta]\\ |x-y|<\eta}} |u(x)-u(y)|. \]

Then

\[ \omega_T(\eta)\to0 \qquad \text{as }\eta\downarrow0. \]

Therefore,

\[ E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|<\eta\}} \right] \le \omega_T(\eta), \]

uniformly for \(0<\theta\le T\).

For the second term,

\[ E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|\ge\eta\}} \right] \le 2\|u\|_\infty P_\theta(|Y_n-\theta|\ge\eta). \]

Now

\[ E_\theta(Y_n)=\theta. \]

Also,

\[ \operatorname{Var}_\theta(X_i(\theta))=\theta^2. \]

Hence

\[ \operatorname{Var}_\theta(S_n)=n\theta^2, \]

and so

\[ \operatorname{Var}_\theta(Y_n) = \operatorname{Var}_\theta\left(\frac{S_n}{n}\right) = \frac{\theta^2}{n}. \]

By Chebyshev’s inequality,

\[ P_\theta(|Y_n-\theta|\ge\eta) \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}_\theta(Y_n)}{\eta^2} = \frac{\theta^2}{n\eta^2}. \]

For \(0<\theta\le T\),

\[ \frac{\theta^2}{n\eta^2} \le \frac{T^2}{n\eta^2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ P_\theta(|Y_n-\theta|\ge\eta) \le \frac{T^2}{n\eta^2}, \]

uniformly for \(0<\theta\le T\).

Combining the two bounds, we get

\[ |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)| \le \omega_T(\eta) + 2\|u\|_\infty \frac{T^2}{n\eta^2}. \]

Taking the supremum over \(0<\theta\le T\),

\[ \sup_{0<\theta\le T} |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)| \le \omega_T(\eta) + 2\|u\|_\infty \frac{T^2}{n\eta^2}. \]

Let \(\varepsilon>0\). First choose \(\eta>0\) such that

\[ \omega_T(\eta)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Then, for this fixed \(\eta\), choose \(n\) large enough such that

\[ 2\|u\|_\infty \frac{T^2}{n\eta^2} < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Therefore, for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ \sup_{0<\theta\le T} |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)| < \varepsilon. \]

Hence

\[ \sup_{0<\theta\le T} \left| \int_0^\infty u(y)e^{-ny\theta^{-1}} \frac{(ny\theta^{-1})^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} n\theta^{-1}\,dy - u(\theta) \right| \to0. \]

Thus the convergence is uniform on finite \(\theta\)-intervals.

The WLLN and inversion of Laplace transforms. Let \(X\ge 0\) be a nonnegative random variable with distribution function \(F\) and Laplace transform

\[ \hat F(\lambda) = E(e^{-\lambda X}) = \int_{[0,\infty)} e^{-\lambda x}F(dx). \]

Show that \(F\) determines \(F\) using the weak law of large numbers and the following steps.

  1. Show

\[ \hat F^{(k)}(\lambda) = \int_{[0,\infty)} (-1)^k x^k e^{-\lambda x}F(dx). \]

  1. Suppose \(\{\xi_n(\theta),n\ge1\}\) are iid non-negative random variables with a Poisson distribution, parameter \(\theta>0\). Use the weak law of large numbers to prove

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty} P\left[ \sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i(\theta)/n\le x \right] = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } x>\theta,\\ 0, & \text{if } x<\theta, \end{cases} \]

and therefore

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{j\le nx} e^{-n\theta}\frac{(n\theta)^j}{j!} = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } x>\theta,\\ 0, & \text{if } x<\theta. \end{cases} \]

  1. Conclude for any \(x\ge0\) which is a point of continuity of \(F\) that

\[ \sum_{j\le nx} \frac{(-1)^j}{j!}n^j\hat F^{(j)}(n) \to F(x). \]

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

(a)

For \(k\ge0\), define

\[ G_k(\lambda) = \int_{[0,\infty)} (-1)^k x^k e^{-\lambda x}F(dx), \qquad \lambda>0. \]

Then

\[ G_0(\lambda)=\hat F(\lambda). \]

We first show that

\[ G_{k-1}'(\lambda)=G_k(\lambda). \]

Fix \(0<a<b<\infty\). Since \(F\) is a probability measure and

\[ \sup_{x\ge0} x^k e^{-ax}<\infty, \]

we have

\[ \int_a^b\int_{[0,\infty)} \left|(-1)^k x^k e^{-\lambda x}\right|F(dx)d\lambda <\infty. \]

Therefore, by Fubini’s theorem,

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_a^b G_k(\lambda)d\lambda &= \int_a^b \int_{[0,\infty)} (-1)^k x^k e^{-\lambda x}F(dx)d\lambda \\ &= \int_{[0,\infty)} \int_a^b (-1)^k x^k e^{-\lambda x}d\lambda F(dx). \end{aligned} \]

For \(k\ge1\),

\[ \int_a^b (-1)^k x^k e^{-\lambda x}d\lambda = \left[ (-1)^{k-1}x^{k-1}e^{-\lambda x} \right]_a^b. \]

Thus

\[ \int_a^b G_k(\lambda)d\lambda = \int_{[0,\infty)} (-1)^{k-1}x^{k-1} \left(e^{-bx}-e^{-ax}\right) F(dx). \]

Hence

\[ \int_a^b G_k(\lambda)d\lambda = G_{k-1}(b)-G_{k-1}(a). \]

Therefore \(G_{k-1}\) is absolutely continuous on every finite interval \([a,b]\subset(0,\infty)\), and

\[ G_{k-1}'(\lambda)=G_k(\lambda) \]

almost everywhere.

Also, each \(G_k\) is continuous. Indeed, if \(\lambda_m\to\lambda>0\), then for all large \(m\),

\[ \lambda_m>\frac{\lambda}{2}, \]

and therefore

\[ x^k e^{-\lambda_m x} \le x^k e^{-(\lambda/2)x}. \]

Since

\[ \sup_{x\ge0} x^k e^{-(\lambda/2)x}<\infty, \]

the dominating function is integrable with respect to \(F(dx)\). By dominated convergence,

\[ G_k(\lambda_m)\to G_k(\lambda). \]

Since \(G_k\) is continuous, the derivative identity holds everywhere. Therefore, by induction,

\[ \hat F^{(k)}(\lambda)=G_k(\lambda). \]

That is,

\[ \hat F^{(k)}(\lambda) = \int_{[0,\infty)} (-1)^k x^k e^{-\lambda x}F(dx). \]

(b)

Let

\[ Y_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i(\theta). \]

Since

\[ E[\xi_i(\theta)]=\theta, \]

the WLLN gives

\[ Y_n\xrightarrow{P}\theta. \]

If \(x>\theta\), then \(x-\theta>0\), so

\[ P(Y_n\le x) = 1-P(Y_n>x) \ge 1-P(|Y_n-\theta|>x-\theta). \]

Since

\[ P(|Y_n-\theta|>x-\theta)\to0, \]

we get

\[ P(Y_n\le x)\to1. \]

If \(x<\theta\), then \(\theta-x>0\), and

\[ P(Y_n\le x) \le P(|Y_n-\theta|\ge \theta-x) \to0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty} P(Y_n\le x) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if }x>\theta,\\ 0, & \text{if }x<\theta. \end{cases} \]

Since the sum of independent Poisson random variables is Poisson,

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i(\theta)\sim \operatorname{Poisson}(n\theta). \]

Hence

\[ P(Y_n\le x) = P\left(\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i(\theta)\le nx\right) = \sum_{j\le nx} e^{-n\theta}\frac{(n\theta)^j}{j!}. \]

Thus

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{j\le nx} e^{-n\theta}\frac{(n\theta)^j}{j!} = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if }x>\theta,\\ 0, & \text{if }x<\theta. \end{cases} \]

(c)

By part (a),

\[ \hat F^{(j)}(n) = \int_{[0,\infty)} (-1)^j y^j e^{-ny}F(dy). \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{(-1)^j}{j!}n^j\hat F^{(j)}(n) = \int_{[0,\infty)} e^{-ny}\frac{(ny)^j}{j!}F(dy). \]

Since the sum over \(j\le nx\) is finite, we may interchange the finite sum and the integral:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{j\le nx} \frac{(-1)^j}{j!}n^j\hat F^{(j)}(n) &= \sum_{j\le nx} \int_{[0,\infty)} e^{-ny}\frac{(ny)^j}{j!}F(dy) \\ &= \int_{[0,\infty)} \sum_{j\le nx} e^{-ny}\frac{(ny)^j}{j!}F(dy). \end{aligned} \]

For fixed \(y\ge0\), the inner sum is

\[ \sum_{j\le nx} e^{-ny}\frac{(ny)^j}{j!}. \]

This is the distribution function of a Poisson random variable with parameter \(ny\). Equivalently, if

\[ \xi_1(y),\dots,\xi_n(y) \]

are iid Poisson random variables with parameter \(y\), then

\[ \sum_{j\le nx} e^{-ny}\frac{(ny)^j}{j!} = P\left[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i(y)\le x \right]. \]

By part (b), for fixed \(y\),

\[ P\left[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i(y)\le x \right] \to \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if }x>y,\\ 0, & \text{if }x<y. \end{cases} \]

Thus the integrand converges to

\[ 1_{\{y<x\}} \]

for every \(y\ne x\).

The integrand is bounded by \(1\). Since \(x\) is a continuity point of \(F\),

\[ F(\{x\})=0. \]

Therefore, by dominated convergence,

\[ \int_{[0,\infty)} \sum_{j\le nx} e^{-ny}\frac{(ny)^j}{j!}F(dy) \to \int_{[0,\infty)} 1_{\{y<x\}}F(dy). \]

But

\[ \int_{[0,\infty)} 1_{\{y<x\}}F(dy) = F(x-). \]

Since \(x\) is a point of continuity of \(F\),

\[ F(x-)=F(x). \]

Therefore,

\[ \sum_{j\le nx} \frac{(-1)^j}{j!}n^j\hat F^{(j)}(n) \to F(x). \]

\(\textbf{Note:}\) For (a), we can also use the definition to prove:For \(k\ge0\), define

\[ G_k(\lambda) = \int_{[0,\infty)} (-1)^k x^k e^{-\lambda x}F(dx), \qquad \lambda>0. \]

Then

\[ G_0(\lambda)=\hat F(\lambda). \]

We show that

\[ G_k'(\lambda)=G_{k+1}(\lambda). \]

For \(h\ne0\),

\[ \frac{G_k(\lambda+h)-G_k(\lambda)}{h} = \int_{[0,\infty)} (-1)^k x^k e^{-\lambda x} \frac{e^{-hx}-1}{h} F(dx). \]

For each fixed \(x\ge0\),

\[ \frac{e^{-hx}-1}{h}\to -x \qquad\text{as }h\to0. \]

Therefore the integrand converges pointwise to

\[ (-1)^{k+1}x^{k+1}e^{-\lambda x}. \]

It remains to justify passing the limit through the integral. Take \(|h|<\lambda/2\). By the mean value theorem,

\[ \left| \frac{e^{-hx}-1}{h} \right| \le x e^{|h|x}. \]

Hence

\[ \left| (-1)^k x^k e^{-\lambda x} \frac{e^{-hx}-1}{h} \right| \le x^{k+1}e^{-(\lambda-|h|)x} \le x^{k+1}e^{-(\lambda/2)x}. \]

Since

\[ \sup_{x\ge0}x^{k+1}e^{-(\lambda/2)x}<\infty, \]

the function

\[ x^{k+1}e^{-(\lambda/2)x} \]

is integrable with respect to the probability measure \(F(dx)\). Therefore, by dominated convergence,

\[ G_k'(\lambda) = \int_{[0,\infty)} (-1)^{k+1}x^{k+1}e^{-\lambda x}F(dx) = G_{k+1}(\lambda). \]

Since \(G_0=\hat F\), induction gives

\[ \hat F^{(k)}(\lambda)=G_k(\lambda). \]

Therefore,

\[ \hat F^{(k)}(\lambda) = \int_{[0,\infty)} (-1)^k x^k e^{-\lambda x}F(dx). \]

Since

\[ \frac{J_n}{n} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n 1_{\{A_i=a_1\}}, \]

the WLLN gives

\[ \frac{J_n}{n}\xrightarrow{P}x. \]

Similarly,

\[ \frac{K_n}{n}\xrightarrow{P}y. \]

Thus

\[ \left(\frac{J_n}{n},\frac{K_n}{n}\right) \xrightarrow{P} (x,y). \]

We now prove that the convergence of expectations is uniform over \(\operatorname{TRI}\).

Since \(\operatorname{TRI}\) is compact and \(u\) is continuous, \(u\) is bounded and uniformly continuous on \(\operatorname{TRI}\). Let

\[ \|u\|_\infty = \sup_{(x,y)\in\operatorname{TRI}}|u(x,y)|. \]

By the union bound,

\[ \begin{aligned} P_{x,y}(A_{n,\delta}^c) &\le P_{x,y}\left( \left|\frac{J_n}{n}-x\right|>\delta \right) \\ &\quad+ P_{x,y}\left( \left|\frac{K_n}{n}-y\right|>\delta \right). \end{aligned} \]

Since \(J_n\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,x)\),

\[ \operatorname{Var}_{x,y}\left(\frac{J_n}{n}\right) = \frac{x(1-x)}{n} \le \frac{1}{4n}. \]

Similarly,

Therefore, by Chebyshev’s inequality,

uniformly over \((x,y)\in\operatorname{TRI}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} &\sup_{(x,y)\in\operatorname{TRI}} \left| E_{x,y}\left[ u\left(\frac{J_n}{n},\frac{K_n}{n}\right) \right] -u(x,y) \right| \\ &\qquad\le \omega(\delta) + \frac{\|u\|_\infty}{n\delta^2}. \end{aligned} \]

Given \(\varepsilon>0\), first choose \(\delta>0\) such that

\[ \omega(\delta)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Then choose \(n\) sufficiently large that

\[ \frac{\|u\|_\infty}{n\delta^2} < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \sup_{(x,y)\in\operatorname{TRI}} \left| E_{x,y}\left[ u\left(\frac{J_n}{n},\frac{K_n}{n}\right) \right] -u(x,y) \right| \to0. \]

Hence

\[ \sum_{j+k\le n} u\left(\frac jn,\frac kn\right) \frac{n!}{j!k!(n-j-k)!} x^jy^k(1-x-y)^{n-j-k} \longrightarrow u(x,y) \]

uniformly on \(\operatorname{TRI}\).

\[ X\in\mathcal C \qquad\text{almost surely,} \]

\[ P(X\le x)=P(X\le a). \]

The Cantor function is constant on \((a,b)\), so

\[ C(x)=C(a). \]

Therefore,

\[ P(X\le x)=C(x) \]

for every \(x\in[0,1]\). Hence \(X\) has the Cantor distribution.

By independence,

\[ \begin{aligned} E(e^{itS_N}) &= \prod_{n=1}^{N} E\left[ \exp\left\{ \frac{itX_n}{3^n} \right\} \right] \\ &= \prod_{n=1}^{N} \left( \frac{1+e^{2it/3^n}}{2} \right). \end{aligned} \]

Since

\[ \frac{1+e^{2iu}}{2} = e^{iu}\cos(u), \]

we have

\[ \begin{aligned} E(e^{itS_N}) &= \prod_{n=1}^{N} e^{it/3^n} \cos\left(\frac{t}{3^n}\right) \\ &= \exp\left\{ it\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac1{3^n} \right\} \prod_{n=1}^{N} \cos\left(\frac{t}{3^n}\right). \end{aligned} \]

Letting \(N\to\infty\),

\[ \phi_X(t) = e^{it/2} \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} \cos\left(\frac{t}{3^n}\right). \]

Thus the Cantor-distributed limit has characteristic function

\[ \phi_X(t) = e^{it/2} \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} \cos\left(\frac{t}{3^n}\right). \]

\[ D_k=S_{2^{k+1}}-S_{2^k}. \]

Then

\[ D_k=X_{2^k+1}+\cdots+X_{2^{k+1}}, \]

so the random variables \(\{D_k\}\) are independent, since they are sums over disjoint blocks of the independent sequence \(\{X_n\}\).

Also,

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{D_k}{2^k} &= \frac{S_{2^{k+1}}-S_{2^k}}{2^k} \\ &= 2\frac{S_{2^{k+1}}}{2^{k+1}} - \frac{S_{2^k}}{2^k} \to0 \end{aligned} \]

almost surely.

Therefore, for every \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[ P\left( \left\{ \frac{|D_k|}{2^k}>\varepsilon \right\} \text{ i.o.} \right) =0. \]

Since the events

\[ \left\{ |D_k|>\varepsilon2^k \right\} \]

are independent, the converse Borel–Cantelli lemma gives

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} P\left( |D_k|>\varepsilon2^k \right) < \infty. \tag{1} \]

If we want to show

\[ \frac{S_n}{n}\to0 \qquad\text{a.s.,} \]

we can consider \(2^k<n\le2^{k+1}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \left|\frac{S_n}{n}\right| &\le \frac{|S_{2^k}|}{2^k} + \frac{|S_n-S_{2^k}|}{2^k}. \end{aligned} \]

By assumption (b), the first term goes to zero almost surely. Thus, we only need to show

\[ \max_{1\le j\le2^k} \frac{|S_{2^k+j}-S_{2^k}|}{2^k} \to0 \qquad\text{a.s.} \tag{2} \]

For \(\varepsilon>0\), let

\[ B_k(\varepsilon) = \left\{ \max_{1\le j\le2^k} |S_{2^k+j}-S_{2^k}| > \varepsilon2^k \right\}. \]

Define

\[ T_{k,j}=S_{2^k+j}-S_{2^k}, \qquad 1\le j\le2^k. \]

Then

\[ T_{k,2^k}=D_k. \]

Using Skorohod’s maximal inequality with

\[ a=b=\varepsilon2^{k-1}, \]

we have

\[ P(B_k(\varepsilon)) \le \frac{ P\left( |D_k|>\varepsilon2^{k-1} \right) }{ 1-C_k }, \tag{3} \]

where

\[ C_k = \max_{1\le j\le2^k} P\left( |D_k-T_{k,j}|>\varepsilon2^{k-1} \right). \]

We need to guarantee that

\[ C_k\to0. \]

Notice that

\[ D_k-T_{k,j} = S_{2^{k+1}}-S_{2^k+j}. \]

Therefore, for each \(j\),

\[ \begin{aligned} &P\left( |S_{2^{k+1}}-S_{2^k+j}| > \varepsilon2^{k-1} \right) \\ &\le P\left( |S_{2^{k+1}}| > \varepsilon2^{k-2} \right) + P\left( |S_{2^k+j}| > \varepsilon2^{k-2} \right). \end{aligned} \tag{4} \]

This is because if

\[ |S_{2^{k+1}}|\le\varepsilon2^{k-2} \]

and

\[ |S_{2^k+j}|\le\varepsilon2^{k-2}, \]

then

\[ |S_{2^{k+1}}-S_{2^k+j}| \le \varepsilon2^{k-1}. \]

For every \(r\in[2^k,2^{k+1}]\),

\[ \varepsilon2^{k-2} \ge \frac{\varepsilon r}{8}. \]

Hence,

\[ P\left( |S_r|>\varepsilon2^{k-2} \right) \le P\left( \left|\frac{S_r}{r}\right|>\frac{\varepsilon}{8} \right). \]

Since

\[ \frac{S_n}{n}\xrightarrow{P}0, \]

we have

\[ \sup_{r\ge2^k} P\left( \left|\frac{S_r}{r}\right|>\frac{\varepsilon}{8} \right) \to0. \]

It follows from (4) that

\[ C_k\to0. \]

Therefore, for all sufficiently large \(k\),

\[ \frac1{1-C_k}\le2. \]

Using (3),

\[ P(B_k(\varepsilon)) \le 2P\left( |D_k|>\varepsilon2^{k-1} \right). \]

By (1), applied with \(\varepsilon/2\),

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} P\left( |D_k|>\varepsilon2^{k-1} \right) < \infty. \]

Thus,

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}P(B_k(\varepsilon))<\infty. \]

By Borel–Cantelli,

\[ P(B_k(\varepsilon)\text{ i.o.})=0. \]

That means that, for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), with probability one,

\[ \max_{1\le j\le2^k} \frac{|S_{2^k+j}-S_{2^k}|}{2^k} \le\varepsilon \]

eventually.

Taking \(\varepsilon\) over the positive rational numbers gives

\[ \max_{1\le j\le2^k} \frac{|S_{2^k+j}-S_{2^k}|}{2^k} \to0 \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

This proves (2).

Finally, for \(2^k<n\le2^{k+1}\),

\[ \left|\frac{S_n}{n}\right| \le \frac{|S_{2^k}|}{2^k} + \max_{1\le j\le2^k} \frac{|S_{2^k+j}-S_{2^k}|}{2^k}. \]

Both terms converge to zero almost surely. Therefore,

\[ \frac{S_n}{n}\to0 \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

\[ \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \left(Y_i'-E(Y_i')\right) \to0 \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nE(Y_i')\to0. \]

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nY_i' \to0 \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-Y_i') \to0 \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nc_iX_i = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nY_i \to0 \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

so

\[ \lambda_n\to\infty. \]

Also, the three series theorem gives

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} E\left(X_n1_{\{X_n\le c\}}\right) < \infty. \]

For each \(n\),

\[ \begin{aligned} E\left(X_n1_{\{X_n\le c\}}\right) &= \int_0^c x\lambda_ne^{-\lambda_nx}\,dx\\ &= \frac{1-(1+c\lambda_n)e^{-c\lambda_n}} {\lambda_n}. \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\lambda_n\to\infty\),

\[ (1+c\lambda_n)e^{-c\lambda_n}\to0. \]

Hence, for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ 1-(1+c\lambda_n)e^{-c\lambda_n} \ge\frac12. \]

Therefore,

\[ E\left(X_n1_{\{X_n\le c\}}\right) \ge \frac1{2\lambda_n} \]

eventually. Thus,

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\lambda_n} < \infty. \]

  • \((\Leftarrow)\)

Conversely, suppose

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\lambda_n}<\infty. \]

We verify the three conditions of the three series theorem.

First,

\[ P(X_n>c)=e^{-c\lambda_n}. \]

To control this series, consider

\[ f(x)=xe^{-cx}, \qquad x>0. \]

Then

\[ f'(x)=e^{-cx}(1-cx). \]

Thus \(f\) reaches its maximum at

\[ x=\frac1c, \]

and

\[ f\left(\frac1c\right)=\frac1{ce}. \]

Therefore,

\[ xe^{-cx}\le\frac1{ce}, \]

so

\[ e^{-cx}\le\frac1{ce}\frac1x. \]

Taking \(x=\lambda_n\),

\[ e^{-c\lambda_n} \le \frac1{ce}\frac1{\lambda_n}. \]

Hence,

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(X_n>c) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-c\lambda_n} \le \frac1{ce} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\lambda_n} < \infty. \]

Second,

\[ E\left(X_n1_{\{X_n\le c\}}\right) \le E(X_n) = \frac1{\lambda_n}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} E\left(X_n1_{\{X_n\le c\}}\right) < \infty. \]

Finally,

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}\left( X_n1_{\{X_n\le c\}} \right) &\le E\left( X_n^2 1_{\{X_n\le c\}} \right)\\ &\le E(X_n^2). \end{aligned} \]

For an exponential random variable,

\[ E(X_n^2) = \operatorname{Var}(X_n)+E(X_n)^2 = \frac2{\lambda_n^2}. \]

Since

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\lambda_n}<\infty, \]

we have \(\lambda_n\to\infty\), so eventually \(\lambda_n\ge1\), and therefore

\[ \frac1{\lambda_n^2} \le \frac1{\lambda_n}. \]

Hence,

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \operatorname{Var}\left( X_n1_{\{X_n\le c\}} \right) \le 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\lambda_n^2} < \infty. \]

Thus all three conditions of the three series theorem hold, and

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}X_n \]

converges almost surely.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}X_n \text{ converges a.s.} \iff \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\lambda_n}<\infty. } \]

Ex 14

Suppose \(\{X_n,n\ge1\}\) are independent, normally distributed with

\[ E(X_n)=\mu_n, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_n)=\sigma_n^2. \]

Show that \(\sum_n X_n\) converges almost surely iff \(\sum_n\mu_n\) converges and \(\sum_n\sigma_n^2<\infty\).

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

First suppose

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}X_n \]

converges almost surely. Let

\[ S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i. \]

Then \(\{S_n\}\) is Cauchy in probability. For \(m<n\), define

\[ Z_{m,n}=S_n-S_m = \sum_{i=m+1}^nX_i. \]

Thus,

\[ Z_{m,n}\xrightarrow{P}0 \qquad\text{as }m,n\to\infty. \]

Since the \(X_i\)’s are independent and normally distributed,

\[ Z_{m,n} \sim N\left( \sum_{i=m+1}^n\mu_i, \sum_{i=m+1}^n\sigma_i^2 \right). \]

Let

\[ a_{m,n} = \sum_{i=m+1}^n\mu_i, \qquad b_{m,n} = \sum_{i=m+1}^n\sigma_i^2. \]

We first show that

\[ b_{m,n}\to0. \]

Suppose not. Then there exists \(\delta>0\) and arbitrarily large \(m<n\) such that

\[ b_{m,n}\ge\delta. \]

The density \(f_{m,n}\) of \(Z_{m,n}\) satisfies

\[ f_{m,n}(x) \le \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi b_{m,n}}} \le \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi\delta}}. \]

Therefore, for any fixed \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} P(|Z_{m,n}|\le\varepsilon) &= \int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon} f_{m,n}(x)\,dx\\ &\le \frac{2\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2\pi\delta}}. \end{aligned} \]

Choose \(\varepsilon>0\) such that

\[ \frac{2\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2\pi\delta}}<1. \]

This contradicts

\[ Z_{m,n}\xrightarrow{P}0, \]

which requires

\[ P(|Z_{m,n}|\le\varepsilon)\to1. \]

Hence,

\[ b_{m,n} = \sum_{i=m+1}^n\sigma_i^2 \to0. \]

Therefore the partial sums of

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_n^2 \]

are Cauchy. Since the terms are nonnegative,

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_n^2<\infty. \]

Next, by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\[ \begin{aligned} P\left( |Z_{m,n}-a_{m,n}|>\varepsilon \right) &\le \frac{b_{m,n}}{\varepsilon^2}\\ &\to0. \end{aligned} \]

Thus,

\[ Z_{m,n}-a_{m,n}\xrightarrow{P}0. \]

Since also

\[ Z_{m,n}\xrightarrow{P}0, \]

we have

\[ a_{m,n} = Z_{m,n}-(Z_{m,n}-a_{m,n}) \xrightarrow{P}0. \]

Since \(a_{m,n}\) is deterministic,

\[ a_{m,n}\to0. \]

That is,

\[ \sum_{i=m+1}^n\mu_i\to0 \qquad\text{as }m,n\to\infty. \]

By the Cauchy criterion,

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_n \]

converges.

Conversely, suppose

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_n \]

converges and

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_n^2<\infty. \]

Write

\[ X_n=(X_n-\mu_n)+\mu_n. \]

The random variables \(\{X_n-\mu_n\}\) are independent and satisfy

\[ E(X_n-\mu_n)=0 \]

and

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X_n-\mu_n) = \sigma_n^2. \]

Since

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \operatorname{Var}(X_n-\mu_n) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_n^2 < \infty, \]

the Kolmogorov convergence criterion gives

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(X_n-\mu_n) \]

converges almost surely.

Since

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_n \]

also converges,

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}X_n = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(X_n-\mu_n) + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_n \]

converges almost surely.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}X_n \text{ converges a.s.} \iff \left[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_n \text{ converges} \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_n^2<\infty \right]. } \]

Ex 15

Prove the three series theorem reduces to a two series theorem when the random variables are positive. If \(V_n\ge0\) are independent, then \(\sum_n V_n<\infty\) a.s. iff for any \(c>0\), we have

\[ \sum_nP[V_n>c]<\infty, \tag{i} \]

\[ \sum_nE(V_n1_{[V_n\le c]})<\infty. \tag{ii} \]

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Suppose first that

\[ \sum_nV_n<\infty \]

almost surely. By the Kolmogorov three series theorem, for any \(c>0\),

\[ \sum_nP(V_n>c)<\infty \]

and

\[ \sum_nE(V_n1_{[V_n\le c]}) \]

converges. Since \(V_n\ge0\),

\[ E(V_n1_{[V_n\le c]})\ge0, \]

so

\[ \sum_nE(V_n1_{[V_n\le c]})<\infty. \]

Conversely, suppose for some \(c>0\),

\[ \sum_nP(V_n>c)<\infty \]

and

\[ \sum_nE(V_n1_{[V_n\le c]})<\infty. \]

Let

\[ V_n'=V_n1_{[V_n\le c]}. \]

Then

\[ \sum_nP(V_n'\ne V_n) = \sum_nP(V_n>c) < \infty. \]

By Borel–Cantelli,

\[ P(V_n'\ne V_n\text{ i.o.})=0, \]

so \(\{V_n'\}\) and \(\{V_n\}\) are tail equivalent.

For \(\sum_nV_n'\), since \(V_n'\ge0\), by Tonelli,

\[ \begin{aligned} E\left(\sum_nV_n'\right) &= \sum_nE(V_n')\\ &= \sum_nE(V_n1_{[V_n\le c]})\\ &<\infty. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \sum_nV_n'<\infty \]

almost surely.

Since \(\{V_n'\}\) and \(\{V_n\}\) are tail equivalent,

\[ \sum_nV_n<\infty \]

almost surely.

Thus, for non-negative independent random variables, the variance series in the three series theorem is redundant.

Ex 17

Suppose \(\{X_n\}\) are independent with

\[ P[X_k=k^2]=\frac1{k^2}, \qquad P[X_k=-1]=1-\frac1{k^2}. \]

Prove

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i \]

exists a.s. and find the limit.

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Let

\[ Y_k=-1 \]

with probability \(1\). Then

\[ P(Y_k\ne X_k) = P(X_k=k^2) = \frac1{k^2}. \]

Hence,

\[ \sum_kP(Y_k\ne X_k) = \sum_k\frac1{k^2} < \infty. \]

By Borel–Cantelli,

\[ P(Y_k\ne X_k\text{ i.o.})=0. \]

Thus \(\{Y_k\}\) and \(\{X_k\}\) are tail equivalent. In particular, almost surely there exists a finite \(N\) such that

\[ X_k=Y_k=-1, \qquad k\ge N. \]

Therefore, for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ \sum_{k=1}^nX_k = \sum_{k=1}^nY_k+C \]

for some finite random constant \(C\).

But

\[ \sum_{k=1}^nY_k=-n\to-\infty. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^nX_k=-\infty } \]

almost surely.

Notes on A Probability Path (Resnick)

Chap 7, Laws of Large Numbers and Sums of Independent Random Variables

Summary

7.1–7.2

From this chapter on, we start to study the asymptotic behaviour of random sums and sample means, such as

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n X_i, \qquad \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i. \]

Unlike the previous chapters, for arbitrary iid random variables \(\{X_n\}\), we may not have finite first or second moments. Therefore, instead of attacking the original sequence directly, we introduce a truncated sequence

\[ X_n \mathbf1_{\{|X_n|\le n\}}. \]

The truncation level is chosen carefully so that the probability of truncation can be controlled.

This naturally introduces the idea of tail equivalence. Even though two random variable sequences may be different,

\[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty P(X_n\neq X_n')<\infty, \]

implies, by the Borel–Cantelli lemma, that they differ only finitely many times with probability one. In other words, they are eventually identical almost surely and therefore share the same asymptotic behaviour.

Proposition 7.1.1 provides the theoretical foundation for this idea. It allows us to first study the sum or sample mean of a truncated random variable sequence, which is usually easier to handle, and then transfer the result back to the original sequence.

In fact, one of the main ideas of this chapter is not to fight heavy tails directly, but to replace a difficult sequence by an asymptotically equivalent and more manageable one.

Section 7.2 develops the Generalized Weak Law of Large Numbers. The truncated variables possess the moment properties needed for the ordinary WLLN, even when the original random variables may not have finite mean or variance.

The proof strategy is actually very simple:

  1. Construct a truncated sequence.
  2. Apply the ordinary WLLN to the truncated sample mean.
  3. Show that the original and truncated sample means are tail equivalent.
  4. Transfer the convergence result back to the original sample mean.

Exercises

Ex 2

Let \(\{X_n\}\) be iid, \(EX_n=\mu\), \(\operatorname{Var}(X_n)=\sigma^2\). Set

\[ \bar X=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n X_i}{n}. \]

Show that

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)^2 \xrightarrow{P} \sigma^2. \]

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

We first use the identity

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)^2 = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2-\bar X^2. \]

Since

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X_1)=\sigma^2<\infty, \]

we have

\[ E(X_1^2)<\infty. \]

Also, since \(\{X_i\}\) are iid, \(\{X_i^2\}\) are iid. Therefore, by Khintchin’s WLLN,

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2 \xrightarrow{P} E(X_1^2). \]

Again by Khintchin’s WLLN,

\[ \bar X = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \xrightarrow{P} E(X_1)=\mu. \]

Since \(f(x)=x^2\) is continuous, we have

\[ \bar X^2 \xrightarrow{P} \mu^2. \]

Hence

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)^2 = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2-\bar X^2 \xrightarrow{P} E(X_1^2)-\mu^2. \]

But

\[ E(X_1^2)-\mu^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X_1) = \sigma^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)^2 \xrightarrow{P} \sigma^2. \]

Ex 3

Occupancy problems. Randomly distribute \(r\) balls in \(n\) boxes so that the sample space \(\Omega\) consists of \(n^r\) equally likely elements. Write

\[ N_n=\sum_{i=1}^n 1_{[\text{\(i\)th box is empty}]} \]

for the number of empty boxes. Check

\[ P[\text{\(i\)th box is empty}] = \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r \]

so that

\[ E(N_n)=n(1-n^{-1})^r. \]

Check that as \(r/n\to c\)

\[ E(N_n)/n\to e^{-c} \]

\[ N_n/n\xrightarrow{P}e^{-c}. \]

For the second result, compute \(\operatorname{Var}(N_n)\) and show \(\operatorname{Var}(N_n/n)\to0\).

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Let

\[ I_i=1_{[\text{\(i\)th box is empty}]}. \]

Then

\[ N_n=\sum_{i=1}^n I_i. \]

For a fixed box \(i\), one ball avoids box \(i\) with probability

\[ 1-\frac1n. \]

Since there are \(r\) balls,

\[ P[I_i=1] = P[\text{\(i\)th box is empty}] = \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r. \]

Therefore,

\[ E(N_n) = E\left(\sum_{i=1}^n I_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n E(I_i) = n\left(1-\frac1n\right)^r. \]

Hence

\[ \frac{E(N_n)}{n} = \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r = \left[\left(1-\frac1n\right)^n\right]^{r/n} \to e^{-c}, \]

since \(r/n\to c\).

Now we compute the variance. Since \(I_i^2=I_i\),

\[ \operatorname{Var}(I_i) = E(I_i^2)-[E(I_i)]^2 = E(I_i)-[E(I_i)]^2. \]

Thus

\[ \operatorname{Var}(I_i) = \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r}. \]

For \(i\ne j\),

\[ E(I_iI_j) = P[I_i=1,I_j=1]. \]

The event \(\{I_i=1,I_j=1\}\) means that both the \(i\)th and \(j\)th boxes are empty. For one ball to avoid both boxes, the probability is

\[ 1-\frac2n. \]

Therefore,

\[ E(I_iI_j) = \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r. \]

So

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(I_i,I_j) = E(I_iI_j)-E(I_i)E(I_j) = \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \operatorname{Var}(N_n) = \operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^n I_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n \operatorname{Var}(I_i) + \sum_{i\ne j}\operatorname{Cov}(I_i,I_j). \]

Hence

\[ \operatorname{Var}(N_n) = n\left[ \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} \right] + n(n-1) \left[ \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} \right]. \]

Dividing by \(n^2\),

\[ \operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{N_n}{n}\right) = \frac1n \left[ \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} \right] + \frac{n-1}{n} \left[ \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} \right]. \]

For the first term,

\[ 0 \le \frac1n \left[ \left(1-\frac1n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} \right] \le \frac1n \to0. \]

For the second term, since \(r/n\to c\),

\[ \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r \to e^{-2c}, \]

and

\[ \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} = \left[\left(1-\frac1n\right)^n\right]^{2r/n} \to e^{-2c}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r - \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} \to0. \]

Thus

\[ \operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{N_n}{n}\right)\to0. \]

By Chebyshev’s inequality, for every \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[ P\left[ \left| \frac{N_n}{n} - \frac{E(N_n)}{n} \right| > \varepsilon \right] \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(N_n/n)}{\varepsilon^2} \to0. \]

So

\[ \frac{N_n}{n} - \frac{E(N_n)}{n} \xrightarrow{P}0. \]

Since

\[ \frac{E(N_n)}{n}\to e^{-c}, \]

we conclude that

\[ \frac{N_n}{n} \xrightarrow{P} e^{-c}. \]

\(\textbf{Note:}\) In this exercise, we cannot apply Khintchin’s WLLN directly to

\[ \frac{N_n}{n} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n I_i, \qquad I_i=1_{[\text{\(i\)th box is empty}]}. \]

Although the \(I_i\)’s have the same distribution, they are not independent. Indeed, for \(i\ne j\),

\[ P(I_i=1,I_j=1) = \left(1-\frac2n\right)^r \ne \left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2r} = P(I_i=1)P(I_j=1). \]

Therefore, instead of using Khintchin’s WLLN directly, we follow the hint: compute \(\operatorname{Var}(N_n)\), show

\[ \operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{N_n}{n}\right)\to0, \]

and then use Chebyshev’s inequality.

Ex 4

Suppose \(g:[0,1]\to \mathbb{R}\) is measurable and Lebesgue integrable. Let \(\{U_n,n\ge 1\}\) be iid uniform random variables and define \(X_i=g(U_i)\). In what sense does \(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i/n\) approximate \(\int_0^1 g(x)dx\)? (This offers a way to approximate the integral by Monte Carlo methods.) How would one guarantee a desired degree of precision?

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

\(\textbf{Pre-learning:}\) By Kolmogorov’s SLLN, if \(\{X_n,n\ge1\}\) are iid and

\[ E|X_1|<\infty, \]

then

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \xrightarrow{a.s.} E(X_1). \]

Now let

\[ X_i=g(U_i), \]

where \(\{U_i,i\ge1\}\) are iid uniform random variables on \([0,1]\).

Since \(g\) is measurable, \(X_i=g(U_i)\) is a random variable. Since the \(U_i\)’s are iid, the \(X_i\)’s are also iid.

Also, since \(g\) is Lebesgue integrable,

\[ E|X_i| = E|g(U_i)| = \int_0^1 |g(x)|\,dx <\infty. \]

Moreover,

\[ E(X_i) = E[g(U_i)] = \int_0^1 g(x)\,dx. \]

Therefore, by Kolmogorov’s SLLN,

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n g(U_i) \xrightarrow{a.s.} E(X_1) = \int_0^1 g(x)\,dx. \]

Thus \(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i/n\) approximates \(\int_0^1 g(x)dx\) in the almost sure sense. Since almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability, we also have

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \xrightarrow{P} \int_0^1 g(x)\,dx. \]

To guarantee a desired degree of precision, we can state the guarantee in an \((\varepsilon,\delta)\) sense. For any \(\varepsilon>0\) and \(\delta>0\), since

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \xrightarrow{P} \int_0^1 g(x)\,dx, \]

there exists \(N>0\) such that for all \(n>N\),

\[ P\left[ \left| \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i - \int_0^1 g(x)\,dx \right| > \varepsilon \right] < \delta. \]

So, by choosing \(n\) sufficiently large, the Monte Carlo approximation can be made accurate within error \(\varepsilon\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\).

\(\textbf{Note:}\) In this exercise, I used a theorem not yet covered in our reading order, namely Theorem 7.5.1. I treat this as a pre-learning step.

Further, the SLLN gives the stronger asymptotic statement, namely almost sure convergence. The \((\varepsilon,\delta)\) statement is the practical probability guarantee. Under only \(g\in L^1[0,1]\), this guarantees the existence of a sufficiently large \(n\), but it does not give an explicit computable formula for \(n\). For an explicit Chebyshev-type bound, one would need a stronger assumption such as

\[ E[g(U)^2]<\infty. \]

Ex 21

Suppose \(\{X_n(\theta), n\ge 1\}\) are iid with common exponential distribution with mean \(\theta\). Then

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n X_i(\theta)/n \xrightarrow{P} \theta. \]

Show for any \(u:\mathbb{R}_+\mapsto \mathbb{R}_+\) which is bounded and continuous that

\[ \int_0^\infty u(y)e^{-ny\theta^{-1}} \frac{(ny\theta^{-1})^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} n\theta^{-1}\,dy \to u(\theta). \]

Show this convergence is uniform on finite \(\theta\)-intervals. (Compare this with the Bernstein polynomials of item 6.3 on page 176.)

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Let

\[ S_n=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i(\theta), \qquad Y_n=\frac{S_n}{n}. \]

Since \(X_i(\theta)\) are iid exponential random variables with mean \(\theta\), we have

\[ S_n\sim \operatorname{Gamma}(n,\theta), \]

with density

\[ f_{S_n}(x) = e^{-x\theta^{-1}} \frac{x^{n-1}\theta^{-n}}{(n-1)!}, \qquad x>0. \]

Now

\[ P(Y_n\le y) = P(S_n\le ny). \]

Therefore the density of \(Y_n\) is

\[ f_{Y_n}(y) = n f_{S_n}(ny). \]

Hence

\[ f_{Y_n}(y) = n e^{-ny\theta^{-1}} \frac{(ny)^{n-1}\theta^{-n}}{(n-1)!}. \]

Since

\[ n(ny)^{n-1}\theta^{-n} = n\theta^{-1}(ny\theta^{-1})^{n-1}, \]

we get

\[ f_{Y_n}(y) = e^{-ny\theta^{-1}} \frac{(ny\theta^{-1})^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} n\theta^{-1}, \qquad y>0. \]

Thus the integral in the exercise is

\[ \int_0^\infty u(y)e^{-ny\theta^{-1}} \frac{(ny\theta^{-1})^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} n\theta^{-1}\,dy = E_\theta[u(Y_n)]. \]

So it is enough to prove

\[ E_\theta[u(Y_n)]\to u(\theta). \]

Since

\[ Y_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i(\theta), \]

and

\[ E_\theta[X_1(\theta)]=\theta, \]

we have

\[ Y_n\xrightarrow{P_\theta}\theta. \]

Fix \(\varepsilon>0\). Since \(u\) is continuous at \(\theta\), there exists \(\delta>0\) such that

\[ |y-\theta|<\delta \]

implies

\[ |u(y)-u(\theta)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)| &= |E_\theta[u(Y_n)-u(\theta)]| \\ &\le E_\theta|u(Y_n)-u(\theta)| \\ &= E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|<\delta\}} \right] \\ &\quad+ E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|\ge \delta\}} \right]. \end{aligned} \]

For the first term,

\[ E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|<\delta\}} \right] \le \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

For the second term, since \(u\) is bounded,

\[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)| \le 2\|u\|_\infty. \]

Therefore

\[ E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|\ge \delta\}} \right] \le 2\|u\|_\infty P_\theta(|Y_n-\theta|\ge \delta). \]

Since

\[ Y_n\xrightarrow{P_\theta}\theta, \]

we have

\[ P_\theta(|Y_n-\theta|\ge \delta)\to0. \]

Thus, for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ 2\|u\|_\infty P_\theta(|Y_n-\theta|\ge \delta) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Hence

\[ |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)|<\varepsilon \]

for sufficiently large \(n\). Therefore

\[ E_\theta[u(Y_n)]\to u(\theta). \]

Equivalently,

\[ \int_0^\infty u(y)e^{-ny\theta^{-1}} \frac{(ny\theta^{-1})^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} n\theta^{-1}\,dy \to u(\theta). \]

Now we prove the convergence is uniform on finite \(\theta\)-intervals. Fix \(T<\infty\). We want to show

\[ \sup_{0<\theta\le T} |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)| \to0. \]

Let \(\eta>0\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)| &\le E_\theta|u(Y_n)-u(\theta)| \\ &= E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|<\eta\}} \right] \\ &\quad+ E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|\ge\eta\}} \right]. \end{aligned} \]

For the first term, if

\[ 0<\theta\le T \]

and

\[ |Y_n-\theta|<\eta, \]

then

\[ Y_n\in[0,T+\eta]. \]

Since \(u\) is continuous, it is uniformly continuous on the compact interval \([0,T+\eta]\). Define

\[ \omega_T(\eta) = \sup_{\substack{x,y\in[0,T+\eta]\\ |x-y|<\eta}} |u(x)-u(y)|. \]

Then

\[ \omega_T(\eta)\to0 \qquad \text{as }\eta\downarrow0. \]

Therefore,

\[ E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|<\eta\}} \right] \le \omega_T(\eta), \]

uniformly for \(0<\theta\le T\).

For the second term,

\[ E_\theta\left[ |u(Y_n)-u(\theta)|1_{\{|Y_n-\theta|\ge\eta\}} \right] \le 2\|u\|_\infty P_\theta(|Y_n-\theta|\ge\eta). \]

Now

\[ E_\theta(Y_n)=\theta. \]

Also,

\[ \operatorname{Var}_\theta(X_i(\theta))=\theta^2. \]

Hence

\[ \operatorname{Var}_\theta(S_n)=n\theta^2, \]

and so

\[ \operatorname{Var}_\theta(Y_n) = \operatorname{Var}_\theta\left(\frac{S_n}{n}\right) = \frac{\theta^2}{n}. \]

By Chebyshev’s inequality,

\[ P_\theta(|Y_n-\theta|\ge\eta) \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}_\theta(Y_n)}{\eta^2} = \frac{\theta^2}{n\eta^2}. \]

For \(0<\theta\le T\),

\[ \frac{\theta^2}{n\eta^2} \le \frac{T^2}{n\eta^2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ P_\theta(|Y_n-\theta|\ge\eta) \le \frac{T^2}{n\eta^2}, \]

uniformly for \(0<\theta\le T\).

Combining the two bounds, we get

\[ |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)| \le \omega_T(\eta) + 2\|u\|_\infty \frac{T^2}{n\eta^2}. \]

Taking the supremum over \(0<\theta\le T\),

\[ \sup_{0<\theta\le T} |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)| \le \omega_T(\eta) + 2\|u\|_\infty \frac{T^2}{n\eta^2}. \]

Let \(\varepsilon>0\). First choose \(\eta>0\) such that

\[ \omega_T(\eta)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Then, for this fixed \(\eta\), choose \(n\) large enough such that

\[ 2\|u\|_\infty \frac{T^2}{n\eta^2} < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Therefore, for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ \sup_{0<\theta\le T} |E_\theta[u(Y_n)]-u(\theta)| < \varepsilon. \]

Hence

\[ \sup_{0<\theta\le T} \left| \int_0^\infty u(y)e^{-ny\theta^{-1}} \frac{(ny\theta^{-1})^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} n\theta^{-1}\,dy - u(\theta) \right| \to0. \]

Thus the convergence is uniform on finite \(\theta\)-intervals.

Ex 22

The WLLN and inversion of Laplace transforms. Let \(X\ge 0\) be a nonnegative random variable with distribution function \(F\) and Laplace transform

\[ \hat F(\lambda) = E(e^{-\lambda X}) = \int_{[0,\infty)} e^{-\lambda x}F(dx). \]

Show that \(F\) determines \(F\) using the weak law of large numbers and the following steps.

  1. Show

\[ \hat F^{(k)}(\lambda) = \int_{[0,\infty)} (-1)^k x^k e^{-\lambda x}F(dx). \]

  1. Suppose \(\{\xi_n(\theta),n\ge1\}\) are iid non-negative random variables with a Poisson distribution, parameter \(\theta>0\). Use the weak law of large numbers to prove

\[ \sum_{1\le j<i\le n}\rho(i-j) = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(n-k)\rho(k). \]

Hence,

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(Y_n) &\le \frac{C}{n} + \frac{2}{n^2} \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(n-k)\rho(k) \\ &\le \frac{C}{n} + \frac{2}{n^2} \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}n\rho(k) \\ &= \frac{C}{n} + \frac{2}{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\rho(k). \end{aligned} \]

Since

\[ \rho(k)\to0, \]

we have

\[ \frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\rho(k)\to0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \operatorname{Var}(Y_n)\to0. \]

Finally, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), Chebyshev’s inequality gives

\[ P(|Y_n|>\varepsilon) = P(|Y_n-E(Y_n)|>\varepsilon) \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(Y_n)}{\varepsilon^2} \to0. \]

Hence,

\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \xrightarrow{P} 0. \]

Notes on A Probability Path (Resnick)

Chap 8, Convergence in Distribution

Summary

Summary 8.1–8.2

The distribution function is completely determined by its values on a dense set. This is the content behind Lemma 8.1.1.

For a proper distribution function \(F\), the different notions in Theorem 8.1.1 are equivalent. In particular,

\[ F_n(I)\to F(I) \]

for every finite interval of continuity \(I=(a,b]\) if and only if

\[ F_n(x)\to F(x), \qquad x\in C(F). \]

Here the important point is that properness prevents probability mass from escaping to \(\pm\infty\).

These are the basic ideas of Section 8.1.

In Section 8.2, Example 8.2.1 illustrates that

\[ X_n \Rightarrow X \]

does not guarantee

\[ F_n(A)\to F(A), \qquad \forall A\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R). \]

This shows that weak convergence only controls certain aspects of the induced probability measures. In terms of the distribution function, we only require convergence at continuity points of the limiting distribution function, or equivalently convergence on finite intervals of continuity. This is much weaker than convergence on every Borel set.

Scheffé’s lemma gives a stronger type of convergence. Suppose the probability measures have densities \(f_n\) and \(f\) with respect to the same dominating measure \(\mu\), and

\[ f_n(x)\to f(x) \qquad \mu\text{-a.e.} \]

Since \(f_n\) and \(f\) are probability densities,

\[ f_n\geq 0,\qquad f\geq 0, \]

and

\[ \int f_n\,d\mu = \int f\,d\mu = 1. \]

These properties allow the pointwise almost-everywhere convergence of the densities to be strengthened to

\[ \int |f_n-f|\,d\mu\to0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \sup_{B\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R)} |F_n(B)-F(B)| \to0. \]

Thus Scheffé’s lemma gives much stronger control over the probability measures than weak convergence.

An important distinction is that Scheffé’s lemma does not require the random variables \(X_n\) and \(X\) to be defined on the same underlying probability space. What is required is that their probability laws admit densities with respect to the same dominating measure \(\mu\).

For example, in the discrete case, counting measure can be used as the common dominating measure, so probability mass functions can be treated as densities.

Exercises

Ex 2

Suppose \(\{X_n, n \geq 1\}\) is a sequence of random variables satisfying

\[ P[X_n=n]=\frac{1}{n}, \]

\[ P[X_n=0]=1-\frac{1}{n}. \]

  1. Does \(\{X_n\}\) converge in probability? If so, to what? Why?

  2. Does \(\{X_n\}\) converge in distribution? If so, to what? Why?

  3. Suppose in addition that \(\{X_n\}\) is an independent sequence. Does \(\{X_n\}\) converge almost surely? What is

\[ \limsup_{n\to\infty}X_n \quad\text{and}\quad \liminf_{n\to\infty}X_n \]

almost surely? Explain your answer.

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

(a)

For any fixed \(\epsilon>0\), when \(n>\epsilon\),

\[ P(|X_n|>\epsilon) = P(X_n=n) = \frac{1}{n} \longrightarrow 0. \]

Therefore,

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{P}0. \]

(b)

Let

\[ F_n(x)=P(X_n\leq x). \]

Then

\[ F_n(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\[4pt] 1-\dfrac{1}{n}, & 0\leq x<n,\\[6pt] 1, & x\geq n. \end{cases} \]

For fixed \(x\in\mathbb{R}\), \(n\) will eventually exceed \(x\). Hence,

\[ F_n(x)\longrightarrow F(x):= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ 1, & x\geq 0. \end{cases} \]

This is the distribution function of the constant random variable \(0\). Therefore,

\[ X_n\Rightarrow 0. \]

(c)

Let

\[ A_n=[X_n>0]=[X_n=n]. \]

Since \(\{X_n\}\) is independent, \(\{A_n\}\) is independent, and

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(A_n) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n} = \infty. \]

By the second Borel–Cantelli lemma,

\[ P(A_n\ \text{i.o.})=1. \]

Thus, almost surely, there exists a subsequence \(\{n_k\}\) such that

\[ X_{n_k}=n_k\longrightarrow\infty. \]

Therefore,

\[ \limsup_{n\to\infty}X_n=\infty \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

Now let

\[ B_n=[X_n=0]. \]

Again, \(\{B_n\}\) is independent, and

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{N}P(B_n) = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right) = N-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n} \longrightarrow\infty. \]

Hence,

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(B_n)=\infty. \]

By the second Borel–Cantelli lemma,

\[ P(B_n\ \text{i.o.})=1. \]

Thus \(X_n=0\) infinitely often almost surely. Since \(X_n\geq0\),

\[ \liminf_{n\to\infty}X_n=0 \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

Therefore,

\[ \limsup_{n\to\infty}X_n=\infty, \qquad \liminf_{n\to\infty}X_n=0 \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

Hence \(\{X_n\}\) does not converge almost surely.

Ex 4

  1. Let \(\{X_n, n \ge 0\}\) be positive integer valued random variables. Prove

\[ X_n \Rightarrow X_0 \]

iff for every \(k \ge 0\)

\[ P[X_n=k] \to P[X_0=k]. \]

  1. Let \(\{X_n\}\) be a sequence of random vectors on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) such that \(X_n\) has a discrete distribution having only points with integer components as possible values. Let \(X\) be another such random vector. Show

\[ X_n \Rightarrow X \]

iff

\[ \sum_x \left|P[X_n=x]-P[X=x]\right| \to 0 \]

as \(n\to\infty\). (Use Scheffé’s lemma.)

  1. For events \(\{A_n,n\ge0\}\), prove

\[ 1_{A_n}\Rightarrow 1_{A_0} \quad\text{iff}\quad P(A_n)\to P(A_0). \]

  1. Let \(F_n\) concentrate all mass at \(x_n\) for \(n\ge0\). Prove

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F_0 \quad\text{iff}\quad x_n\to x_0. \]

  1. Let \(X_n=1-1/n\) or \(1+1/n\) each with probability \(1/2\) and suppose \(P[X=1]=1\). Show \(X_n\Rightarrow X\) but that the mass function \(f_n(x)\) of \(X_n\) does not converge for any \(x\).

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

(a)

Let \(F_n\) be the df of \(X_n\).

Suppose first that

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X_0. \]

Since the random variables are integer valued, for every \(k\ge0\) the points

\[ k-\frac12,\qquad k+\frac12 \]

are continuity points of \(F_0\). Hence

\[ F_n\left(k-\frac12\right) \to F_0\left(k-\frac12\right) \]

and

\[ F_n\left(k+\frac12\right) \to F_0\left(k+\frac12\right). \]

But

\[ P(X_n=k) = F_n\left(k+\frac12\right) - F_n\left(k-\frac12\right), \]

so

\[ P(X_n=k)\to P(X_0=k). \]

Conversely, suppose that for every \(k\ge0\),

\[ P(X_n=k)\to P(X_0=k). \]

For any fixed \(x\in\mathbb{R}\),

\[ F_n(x) = \sum_{k\le x}P(X_n=k). \]

Since the random variables are positive integer valued, this is a finite sum for fixed \(x\). Therefore

\[ F_n(x) \to \sum_{k\le x}P(X_0=k) = F_0(x). \]

In particular, this holds at every continuity point of \(F_0\), and hence

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X_0. \]

(b)

Suppose first that

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X. \]

For each

\[ x=(x_1,\ldots,x_d)\in\mathbb{Z}^d, \]

define

\[ B_x = \prod_{i=1}^d \left(x_i-\frac12,x_i+\frac12\right]. \]

Since \(X_n\) and \(X\) can take only values with integer components,

\[ P(X_n\in B_x)=P(X_n=x) \]

and

\[ P(X\in B_x)=P(X=x). \]

The probability of \(B_x\) can be expressed by inclusion-exclusion as a finite alternating sum of the probabilities of \(2^d\) lower orthants. The corners of these lower orthants have coordinates of the form

\[ x_i\pm\frac12. \]

Since \(X\) is integer valued, all these corners are continuity points of its distribution function. Therefore, from \(X_n\Rightarrow X\),

\[ P(X_n\in B_x)\to P(X\in B_x), \]

and hence

\[ P(X_n=x)\to P(X=x) \qquad \text{for every }x\in\mathbb{Z}^d. \]

Now define

\[ p_n(x)=P(X_n=x), \qquad p(x)=P(X=x). \]

These are densities with respect to counting measure on \(\mathbb{Z}^d\), and

\[ \sum_xp_n(x)=\sum_xp(x)=1. \]

Since

\[ p_n(x)\to p(x) \]

pointwise, Scheffé’s lemma applied with respect to counting measure gives

\[ \sum_x|p_n(x)-p(x)|\to0. \]

Therefore

\[ \sum_x \left| P(X_n=x)-P(X=x) \right| \to0. \]

Conversely, suppose

\[ \sum_x \left| P(X_n=x)-P(X=x) \right| \to0. \]

For any \(y\in\mathbb{R}^d\),

\[ \begin{aligned} |F_n(y)-F(y)| &= \left| \sum_{x\le y} \left\{P(X_n=x)-P(X=x)\right\} \right|\\ &\le \sum_x \left| P(X_n=x)-P(X=x) \right| \to0. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(F_n(y)\to F(y)\), in particular at every continuity point of \(F\), and hence

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X. \]

(c)

Let

\[ X_n=1_{A_n}, \qquad X_0=1_{A_0}, \]

and write

\[ p_n=P(A_n), \qquad p_0=P(A_0). \]

Suppose

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X_0. \]

Since \(1/2\) is a continuity point of the df of \(X_0\),

\[ F_n\left(\frac12\right) \to F_0\left(\frac12\right). \]

But

\[ F_n\left(\frac12\right) = P(X_n=0) = 1-p_n, \]

and similarly

\[ F_0\left(\frac12\right)=1-p_0. \]

Therefore

\[ p_n\to p_0, \]

that is,

\[ P(A_n)\to P(A_0). \]

Conversely, suppose

\[ P(A_n)\to P(A_0), \]

or equivalently,

\[ p_n\to p_0. \]

The df of \(X_n\) is

\[ F_n(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ 1-p_n, & 0\le x<1,\\ 1, & x\ge1. \end{cases} \]

Thus \(p_n\to p_0\) implies

\[ F_n(x)\to F_0(x) \]

for every \(x\), and hence

\[ 1_{A_n}\Rightarrow1_{A_0}. \]

(d)

Suppose first that

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F_0. \]

Assume, to the contrary, that

\[ x_n\not\to x_0. \]

Then there exists \(\epsilon_0>0\) and a subsequence \(\{x_{n_k}\}\) such that, after possibly taking another subsequence, either

\[ x_{n_k}\ge x_0+\epsilon_0 \]

for every \(k\), or

\[ x_{n_k}\le x_0-\epsilon_0 \]

for every \(k\).

In the first case, choose

\[ y=x_0+\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}. \]

Then \(y\ne x_0\), so \(y\) is a continuity point of \(F_0\), but

\[ F_{n_k}(y)=0 \]

while

\[ F_0(y)=1, \]

which contradicts \(F_{n_k}(y)\to F_0(y)\).

Similarly, in the second case choose

\[ y=x_0-\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}. \]

Then

\[ F_{n_k}(y)=1 \]

while

\[ F_0(y)=0, \]

again a contradiction.

Therefore

\[ x_n\to x_0. \]

Conversely, suppose

\[ x_n\to x_0. \]

The only discontinuity point of \(F_0\) is \(x_0\).

If \(x<x_0\), then for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ x<x_n, \]

so

\[ F_n(x)=0=F_0(x). \]

If \(x>x_0\), then for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ x>x_n, \]

so

\[ F_n(x)=1=F_0(x). \]

Hence \(F_n(x)\to F_0(x)\) at every continuity point of \(F_0\), and therefore

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F_0. \]

(e)

The df of \(X\) is

\[ F(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x<1,\\ 1, & x\ge1. \end{cases} \]

Thus \(1\) is the only discontinuity point of \(F\).

For any fixed \(x<1\), when \(n\) is sufficiently large,

\[ x<1-\frac1n, \]

and therefore

\[ F_n(x)=0=F(x). \]

For any fixed \(x>1\), when \(n\) is sufficiently large,

\[ x>1+\frac1n, \]

and therefore

\[ F_n(x)=1=F(x). \]

Hence

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X. \]

The mass function of \(X_n\) is

\[ f_n(x) = \begin{cases} \frac12, & x=1-\frac1n,\\ \frac12, & x=1+\frac1n,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \]

while the mass function of \(X\) is

\[ f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x=1,\\ 0, & x\ne1. \end{cases} \]

At \(x=1\),

\[ f_n(1)=0 \]

for every \(n\), whereas

\[ f(1)=1. \]

Thus

\[ f_n(1)\not\to f(1). \]

In fact, for every fixed \(x\),

\[ f_n(x)\to0. \]

Therefore the literal statement in the exercise that \(f_n(x)\) ``does not converge for any \(x\)’’ is not correct: the mass functions converge pointwise to the zero function, but they do not converge pointwise to the mass function \(f\) of the limiting random variable.

Ex 12

Suppose \(F_n\) has density

\[ f_n(x)= \begin{cases} 1-\cos 2n\pi x, & \text{if }0\le x\le 1,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

Show that \(F_n\) converges weakly to the uniform distribution on \([0,1]\) but that the densities \(f_n\) do not converge.

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

For \(0\le x\le 1\),

\[ F_n(x) = \int_0^x(1-\cos 2n\pi t)\,dt = x-\frac{1}{2n\pi}\sin(2n\pi x). \]

Therefore,

\[ F_n(x)\to x. \]

Also, for \(x<0\),

\[ F_n(x)=0, \]

and for \(x>1\),

\[ F_n(x)=1. \]

Hence, if \(F\) is the distribution function of the uniform distribution on \([0,1]\),

\[ F(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ x, & 0\le x\le1,\\ 1, & x>1, \end{cases} \]

then

\[ F_n(x)\to F(x) \]

for every \(x\). Therefore,

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F. \]

However, take the fixed point

\[ x=\frac12. \]

Then

\[ f_n\left(\frac12\right) = 1-\cos(n\pi) = 1-(-1)^n, \]

so

\[ f_n\left(\frac12\right) = \begin{cases} 0, & n\text{ even},\\ 2, & n\text{ odd}. \end{cases} \]

Thus \(f_n(1/2)\) does not converge, and hence the densities \(f_n\) do not converge pointwise.

Ex 18

Give an example of a sequence of discrete distributions that converge weakly to a limit distribution which possesses a density.

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Let \(X_n\) be a random variable that puts mass \(1/n\) uniformly on

\[ \left\{ \frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\ldots,\frac{n}{n} \right\}. \]

That is,

\[ P\left(X_n=\frac{k}{n}\right)=\frac1n, \qquad k=1,\ldots,n. \]

For \(0\le x\le1\), the distribution function of \(X_n\) is

\[ F_n(x) = \frac{\lfloor nx\rfloor}{n}. \]

Let \(F\) be the distribution function of the uniform distribution on \([0,1]\):

\[ F(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ x, & 0\le x\le1,\\ 1, & x>1. \end{cases} \]

For \(0\le x\le1\),

\[ 0 \le x-\frac{\lfloor nx\rfloor}{n} < \frac1n, \]

and therefore

\[ F_n(x) = \frac{\lfloor nx\rfloor}{n} \to x = F(x). \]

For \(x<0\) and \(x>1\),

\[ F_n(x)=F(x). \]

Thus,

\[ F_n(x)\to F(x) \qquad \text{for every }x\in\mathbb R, \]

so

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X, \]

where \(X\sim U(0,1)\).

The limiting distribution possesses the density

\[ f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & 0<x<1,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

Hence a sequence of discrete distributions can converge weakly to a distribution possessing a density.

\(\textbf{Note:}\) In basic probability and statistics, we usually take the central limit theorem result

\[ \frac{B_n-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\Rightarrow N(0,1), \qquad B_n\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p), \]

as a familiar fact. This would also provide an example of discrete distributions converging weakly to a distribution possessing a density. However, since we have not yet learned the CLT in this book, we use the discrete-uniform-grid example above instead.

Ex 20

Suppose we have independent Bernoulli trials where the probability of success in a trial is \(p\). Let \(V_p\) be the geometrically distributed number of trials needed to get the first success so that

\[ P[V_p \ge n]=(1-p)^{n-1},\qquad n\ge1. \]

Show as \(p\downarrow0\)

\[ pV_p\Rightarrow E, \]

where \(E\) is a unit exponential random variable.

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

For \(x\ge0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} P(pV_p>x) &= P\left(V_p>\frac{x}{p}\right)\\ &= P\left( V_p\ge \left\lfloor\frac{x}{p}\right\rfloor+1 \right)\\ &= (1-p)^{\lfloor x/p\rfloor}. \end{aligned} \]

Now,

\[ \log\left\{(1-p)^{\lfloor x/p\rfloor}\right\} = p\left\lfloor\frac{x}{p}\right\rfloor \frac{\log(1-p)}{p}. \]

As \(p\downarrow0\),

\[ p\left\lfloor\frac{x}{p}\right\rfloor\to x \]

and

\[ \frac{\log(1-p)}{p}\to-1. \]

Therefore,

\[ (1-p)^{\lfloor x/p\rfloor} \to e^{-x}. \]

Hence, for \(x\ge0\),

\[ P(pV_p>x)\to e^{-x}=P(E>x). \]

For \(x<0\),

\[ P(pV_p>x)=1=P(E>x). \]

Thus the survival functions converge to that of a unit exponential random variable, and therefore

\[ pV_p\Rightarrow E. \]

Ex 21

Sampling with replacement. Let \(\{X_n, n \ge 1\}\) be iid and uniformly distributed on the set \(\{1,\ldots,m\}\). In repeated sampling, let \(V_m\) be the time of the first coincidence; that is, the time when we first get a repeated outcome

\[ V_m:=\inf\{n\ge2:X_n\in\{X_1,\ldots,X_{n-1}\}\}. \]

Verify that

\[ P[V_m>n] = \prod_{i=2}^n \left(1-\frac{i-1}{m}\right). \]

Show as \(m\to\infty\) that

\[ V_m/\sqrt m\Rightarrow V \]

where

\[ P[V>x]=\exp\{-x^2/2\},\qquad x>0. \]

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

The event

\[ [V_m>n] \]

means that the first \(n\) samples are all different.

The first sample can take any value. For the second sample, there are \(m-1\) possible values different from the first one; for the third sample, there are \(m-2\) possible values different from the first two; and so on. Therefore,

\[ P(V_m>n) = \frac{m-1}{m} \frac{m-2}{m} \cdots \frac{m-n+1}{m}, \]

so

\[ P(V_m>n) = \prod_{i=2}^n \left(1-\frac{i-1}{m}\right). \]

Now fix \(x>0\) and let

\[ N_m=\lfloor x\sqrt m\rfloor. \]

Since \(V_m\) is integer valued,

\[ \begin{aligned} P\left(\frac{V_m}{\sqrt m}>x\right) &= P(V_m>x\sqrt m)\\ &= P(V_m>N_m)\\ &= \prod_{j=1}^{N_m-1} \left(1-\frac{j}{m}\right). \end{aligned} \]

Write

\[ L_m = P\left(\frac{V_m}{\sqrt m}>x\right). \]

Then

\[ \log L_m = \sum_{j=1}^{N_m-1} \log\left(1-\frac{j}{m}\right). \]

Since

\[ \max_{1\le j\le N_m-1}\frac{j}{m} \le \frac{N_m}{m} \to0, \]

we may use

\[ \log(1-u) = -u+O(u^2) \]

uniformly over the terms in the sum. Hence

\[ \log L_m = -\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^{N_m-1}j + O\left( \frac1{m^2} \sum_{j=1}^{N_m-1}j^2 \right). \]

For the first term,

\[ -\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^{N_m-1}j = -\frac{N_m(N_m-1)}{2m}. \]

Since

\[ \frac{N_m}{\sqrt m}\to x, \]

we have

\[ -\frac{N_m(N_m-1)}{2m} \to -\frac{x^2}{2}. \]

For the second term,

\[ \frac1{m^2} \sum_{j=1}^{N_m-1}j^2 = O\left(\frac{N_m^3}{m^2}\right). \]

Since \(N_m\) is of order \(\sqrt m\),

\[ \frac{N_m^3}{m^2} = O\left(m^{-1/2}\right) \to0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \log L_m \to -\frac{x^2}{2}, \]

and hence

\[ P\left(\frac{V_m}{\sqrt m}>x\right) \to \exp\left\{-\frac{x^2}{2}\right\}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{V_m}{\sqrt m}\Rightarrow V, } \]

where

\[ P(V>x) = \exp\left\{-\frac{x^2}{2}\right\}, \qquad x>0. \]

Ex 32

Given random variables \(\{X_n\}\) such that \(0\le X_n\le 1\). Suppose for all \(x\in(0,1)\)

\[ P[X_n\le x]\to 1-p. \]

Show \(X_n\Rightarrow B\) where \(B\) is a Bernoulli random variable with success probability \(p\).

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

The df of \(B\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)\) is

\[ F_B(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ 1-p, & 0\le x<1,\\ 1, & x\ge1. \end{cases} \]

For every \(x\in(0,1)\), by assumption,

\[ F_n(x) = P(X_n\le x) \to 1-p = F_B(x). \]

Since

\[ 0\le X_n\le1, \]

for every \(n\),

\[ F_n(x)=0=F_B(x), \qquad x<0, \]

and

\[ F_n(x)=1=F_B(x), \qquad x>1. \]

Thus \(F_n(x)\to F_B(x)\) at every continuity point of \(F_B\), except that when \(p=1\), \(x=0\) is also a continuity point. In this case, for any fixed \(y\in(0,1)\),

\[ 0\le F_n(0)\le F_n(y)\to0, \]

so

\[ F_n(0)\to0=F_B(0). \]

Therefore, in all cases,

\[ F_n(x)\to F_B(x) \]

for every \(x\in C(F_B)\), and hence

\[ X_n\Rightarrow B. \]

Ex 8

Suppose \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are iid exponentially distributed with mean 1. Let

\[ X_{1,n}<\cdots<X_{n,n} \]

be the order statistics. Fix an integer \(l\) and show

\[ nX_{l,n}\Rightarrow Y_l \]

where \(Y_l\) has a gamma\((l,1)\) distribution.

Try doing this (a) in a straightforward way by brute force and then (b) try using the Renyi representation for the spacings of order statistics from the exponential density. See Exercise 32 on page 116.

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

(a)

For \(t>0\), define

\[ N_n(t) = \#\{i:X_i\le t\}. \]

The event

\[ [X_{l,n}\le t] \]

occurs if and only if at least \(l\) of \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are less than or equal to \(t\). Thus,

\[ [X_{l,n}\le t] = [N_n(t)\ge l]. \]

Since \(X_i\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1)\),

\[ P(X_i\le t)=1-e^{-t}, \]

so

\[ N_n(t)\sim \operatorname{Bin}\left(n,1-e^{-t}\right). \]

Therefore, for \(x>0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} P(nX_{l,n}\le x) &= P\left(X_{l,n}\le\frac{x}{n}\right)\\ &= P\left(N_n\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\ge l\right). \end{aligned} \]

For each fixed \(k\),

\[ \begin{aligned} P\left(N_n\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)=k\right) &= \binom{n}{k} \left(1-e^{-x/n}\right)^k \left(e^{-x/n}\right)^{n-k}\\ &= \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k!} \left(1-e^{-x/n}\right)^k e^{-x(1-k/n)}. \end{aligned} \]

Rewrite the first two factors as

\[ \frac{1}{k!} \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} \left[ \frac{n-j}{n} \cdot n\left(1-e^{-x/n}\right) \right]. \]

Since \(k\) is fixed,

\[ \frac{n-j}{n}\to1, \qquad j=0,\ldots,k-1, \]

and

\[ n\left(1-e^{-x/n}\right)\to x. \]

Also,

\[ e^{-x(1-k/n)}\to e^{-x}. \]

Hence

\[ P\left(N_n\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)=k\right) \to e^{-x}\frac{x^k}{k!} = P(\operatorname{Poi}(x)=k). \]

Now,

\[ \begin{aligned} P(nX_{l,n}\le x) &= P\left(N_n\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\ge l\right)\\ &= 1- \sum_{k=0}^{l-1} P\left(N_n\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)=k\right). \end{aligned} \]

Since this is a finite sum,

\[ P(nX_{l,n}\le x) \to 1- \sum_{k=0}^{l-1} e^{-x}\frac{x^k}{k!}. \]

But if

\[ Y_l\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(l,1), \]

then

\[ P(Y_l\le x) = 1- \sum_{k=0}^{l-1} e^{-x}\frac{x^k}{k!}. \]

Therefore,

\[ nX_{l,n}\Rightarrow Y_l. \]

(b)

Using the Renyi representation, write

\[ X_{l,n} = X_{1,n} + (X_{2,n}-X_{1,n}) +\cdots+ (X_{l,n}-X_{l-1,n}). \]

Thus,

\[ X_{l,n} = \sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\xi_{i,n}, \]

where the \(\xi_{i,n}\) are independent and

\[ \xi_{i,n}\sim\operatorname{Exp}(n-i). \]

Let

\[ Y_{i,n}=n\xi_{i,n}. \]

Then

\[ Y_{i,n} \sim \operatorname{Exp}\left(\frac{n-i}{n}\right) = \operatorname{Exp}\left(1-\frac{i}{n}\right). \]

Hence the density of \(Y_{i,n}\) is

\[ f_{i,n}(y) = \left(1-\frac{i}{n}\right) \exp\left\{ -\left(1-\frac{i}{n}\right)y \right\} 1_{\{y>0\}}. \]

For each fixed \(i=0,\ldots,l-1\),

\[ f_{i,n}(y) \to e^{-y}1_{\{y>0\}}. \]

Since \(Y_{0,n},\ldots,Y_{l-1,n}\) are independent, their joint density is

\[ g_n(y_0,\ldots,y_{l-1}) = \prod_{i=0}^{l-1} f_{i,n}(y_i). \]

Therefore,

\[ g_n(y_0,\ldots,y_{l-1}) \to \prod_{i=0}^{l-1} e^{-y_i}1_{\{y_i>0\}} = :g(y_0,\ldots,y_{l-1}). \]

Both \(g_n\) and \(g\) are densities on \(\mathbb{R}^l\). By Scheffé’s lemma,

\[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^l} |g_n-g|\,dy \to0. \]

For \(x>0\), define

\[ A_x = \left\{ (y_0,\ldots,y_{l-1}): \sum_{i=0}^{l-1}y_i\le x \right\}. \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} & \left| P\left( \sum_{i=0}^{l-1}Y_{i,n}\le x \right) - P\left( \sum_{i=0}^{l-1}Y_i\le x \right) \right|\\ &\qquad= \left| \int_{A_x}(g_n-g)\,dy \right|\\ &\qquad\le \int_{\mathbb{R}^l} |g_n-g|\,dy \to0, \end{aligned} \]

where \(Y_0,\ldots,Y_{l-1}\) are iid \(\operatorname{Exp}(1)\).

Therefore,

\[ \sum_{i=0}^{l-1}Y_{i,n} \Rightarrow \sum_{i=0}^{l-1}Y_i. \]

But

\[ \sum_{i=0}^{l-1}Y_i \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(l,1), \]

and

\[ \sum_{i=0}^{l-1}Y_{i,n} = nX_{l,n}. \]

Hence,

\[ nX_{l,n} \Rightarrow \operatorname{Gamma}(l,1). \]

Ex 22

Sample median; more order statistics. Let \(U_1,\ldots,U_n\) be iid \(U(0,1)\) random variables and consider the order statistics

\[ U_{1,n}\le U_{2,n}\le\cdots\le U_{n,n}. \]

When \(n\) is odd, the middle order statistic is the sample median. Show that

\[ 2\left(U_{n+1,2n+1}-\frac12\right)\sqrt{2n} \]

has a limit distribution. What is it? (Hint: Use Scheffe’s lemma 8.2.1 page 253.)

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Intuitively, from the result and framework of Section 8.2.1, we guess that the limit should be standard normal. We now follow the same framework to verify it.

For the order statistic \(U_{k,n}\), consider the event

\[ [U_{k,n}\le x]. \]

This means that at least \(k\) of \(U_1,\ldots,U_n\) are less than or equal to \(x\).

Define

\[ N_n(x)=\#\{i:U_i\le x\}. \]

Then

\[ [U_{k,n}\le x] \iff [N_n(x)\ge k]. \]

Since the \(U_i\)’s are iid \(U(0,1)\),

\[ N_n(x)\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,x). \]

Therefore the cdf of \(U_{k,n}\) is

\[ P(U_{k,n}\le x) = P(N_n(x)\ge k) = \sum_{i=k}^n \binom ni x^i(1-x)^{n-i}. \]

Differentiating, we obtain the density

\[ f_{k,n}(x) = \frac{n!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!} x^{k-1}(1-x)^{n-k}, \qquad 0<x<1. \]

For our question, substitute \(k=n+1\) and the sample size \(2n+1\). Thus

\[ f_{n+1,2n+1}(u) = \frac{(2n+1)!}{(n!)^2} u^n(1-u)^n, \qquad 0<u<1. \]

Now let

\[ Y_n = 2\sqrt{2n} \left( U_{n+1,2n+1}-\frac12 \right). \]

Then

\[ u = \frac12+\frac{x}{2\sqrt{2n}}, \]

and

\[ \frac{du}{dx} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2n}}. \]

Therefore the density of \(Y_n\) is

\[ g_n(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2n}} f_{n+1,2n+1} \left( \frac12+\frac{x}{2\sqrt{2n}} \right), \]

for \(|x|<\sqrt{2n}\), and \(g_n(x)=0\) otherwise.

Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} g_n(x) &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2n}} \frac{(2n+1)!}{(n!)^2} \left( \frac12+\frac{x}{2\sqrt{2n}} \right)^n \left( \frac12-\frac{x}{2\sqrt{2n}} \right)^n\\ &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2n}} \frac{(2n+1)!}{(n!)^2} \left(\frac14\right)^n \left( 1-\frac{x^2}{2n} \right)^n. \end{aligned} \]

By Stirling’s formula,

\[ n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n} \left(\frac ne\right)^n, \]

and

\[ (2n)! \sim \sqrt{4\pi n} \left(\frac{2n}{e}\right)^{2n}. \]

Therefore the constant part satisfies

\[ \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2n}} \frac{(2n+1)!}{(n!)^2} \left(\frac14\right)^n \to \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}. \]

Also, for every fixed \(x\),

\[ \left( 1-\frac{x^2}{2n} \right)^n \to e^{-x^2/2}. \]

Thus

\[ g_n(x) \to \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}, \]

which is the standard normal density.

Since \(g_n\) and the standard normal density are probability densities, Scheffé’s lemma gives convergence of the corresponding distributions. Therefore

\[ 2\sqrt{2n} \left( U_{n+1,2n+1}-\frac12 \right) \Rightarrow Z, \]

where

\[ Z\sim N(0,1). \]

Ex 19

(Second continuous mapping theorem) Suppose that \(X_n\Rightarrow X_0\) and that for \(n\ge0\), \(\chi_n:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\) are measurable. Define

\[ E:=\{x:\exists x_n\to x\text{ but }\chi_n(x_n)\not\to\chi_0(x)\}. \]

Suppose \(E\) is measurable and \(P[X_0\in E]=0\). Show

\[ \chi_n(X_n)\Rightarrow\chi_0(X_0). \]

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

By the Baby Skorohod theorem, there exist random variables \(X_n^\#\), \(n\ge0\), defined on \(((0,1],\mathcal{B}((0,1]),\lambda)\) such that

\[ X_n^\#\overset{d}{=}X_n \]

for every \(n\ge0\), and

\[ X_n^\#\to X_0^\# \qquad \lambda\text{-a.s.} \]

Since \(\chi_n\) are measurable, \(\chi_n(X_n^\#)\) and \(\chi_n(X_n)\) are measurable, and

\[ \chi_n(X_n^\#)\overset{d}{=}\chi_n(X_n). \]

Let

\[ A = \{t\in(0,1]:X_n^\#(t)\to X_0^\#(t)\}. \]

By Baby Skorohod,

\[ \lambda(A)=1. \]

Also, since

\[ X_0^\#\overset{d}{=}X_0 \]

and

\[ P(X_0\in E)=0, \]

we have

\[ \lambda\{t:X_0^\#(t)\in E^c\} = P(X_0\in E^c) = 1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \lambda\left( A\cap \{t:X_0^\#(t)\in E^c\} \right) = 1. \]

For every \(t\) in this intersection,

\[ X_n^\#(t)\to X_0^\#(t), \]

and

\[ X_0^\#(t)\notin E. \]

By the definition of \(E\), this implies

\[ \chi_n(X_n^\#(t)) \to \chi_0(X_0^\#(t)). \]

Hence

\[ \chi_n(X_n^\#) \to \chi_0(X_0^\#) \qquad \lambda\text{-a.s.} \]

Therefore,

\[ \chi_n(X_n^\#) \Rightarrow \chi_0(X_0^\#). \]

Finally,

\[ \chi_n(X_n^\#)\overset{d}{=}\chi_n(X_n) \]

and

\[ \chi_0(X_0^\#)\overset{d}{=}\chi_0(X_0), \]

so

\[ \boxed{ \chi_n(X_n)\Rightarrow\chi_0(X_0). } \] \(\textbf{Note:}\) We should notice that in the book, Corollary 8.3.1 (Continuous Mapping Theorem) does not explicitly state that the function/mapping \(h\) is measurable, even though the following remark states that \(\operatorname{Disc}(h)\) is always measurable, even if \(h\) itself is not measurable. However, in Exercise 19, the mappings \(\chi_n\) are explicitly assumed to be measurable. Thus, in Corollary 8.3.1, some measurability condition is still implicitly required: at least \(h(X_n)\) and \(h(X_0)\) must be measurable random variables. A sufficient condition is that \(h\) is Borel measurable on the relevant state space. The measurability of \(\operatorname{Disc}(h)\) only guarantees that the condition \(P[X_0\in\operatorname{Disc}(h)]=0\) is well-defined; it does not by itself guarantee that \(h(X_n)\) is measurable.

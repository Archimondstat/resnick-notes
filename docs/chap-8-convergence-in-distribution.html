Chap 8, Convergence in Distribution

Summary

Summary 8.1–8.2

The distribution function is completely determined by its values on a dense set. This is the content behind Lemma 8.1.1.

For a proper distribution function \(F\), the different notions in Theorem 8.1.1 are equivalent. In particular,

\[ F_n(I)\to F(I) \]

for every finite interval of continuity \(I=(a,b]\) if and only if

\[ F_n(x)\to F(x), \qquad x\in C(F). \]

Here the important point is that properness prevents probability mass from escaping to \(\pm\infty\).

These are the basic ideas of Section 8.1.

In Section 8.2, Example 8.2.1 illustrates that

\[ X_n \Rightarrow X \]

does not guarantee

\[ F_n(A)\to F(A), \qquad \forall A\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R). \]

This shows that weak convergence only controls certain aspects of the induced probability measures. In terms of the distribution function, we only require convergence at continuity points of the limiting distribution function, or equivalently convergence on finite intervals of continuity. This is much weaker than convergence on every Borel set.

Scheffé’s lemma gives a stronger type of convergence. Suppose the probability measures have densities \(f_n\) and \(f\) with respect to the same dominating measure \(\mu\), and

\[ f_n(x)\to f(x) \qquad \mu\text{-a.e.} \]

Since \(f_n\) and \(f\) are probability densities,

\[ f_n\geq 0,\qquad f\geq 0, \]

and

\[ \int f_n\,d\mu = \int f\,d\mu = 1. \]

These properties allow the pointwise almost-everywhere convergence of the densities to be strengthened to

\[ \int |f_n-f|\,d\mu\to0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \sup_{B\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R)} |F_n(B)-F(B)| \to0. \]

Thus Scheffé’s lemma gives much stronger control over the probability measures than weak convergence.

An important distinction is that Scheffé’s lemma does not require the random variables \(X_n\) and \(X\) to be defined on the same underlying probability space. What is required is that their probability laws admit densities with respect to the same dominating measure \(\mu\).

For example, in the discrete case, counting measure can be used as the common dominating measure, so probability mass functions can be treated as densities.

Exercises

Ex 2

Suppose \(\{X_n, n \geq 1\}\) is a sequence of random variables satisfying

\[ P[X_n=n]=\frac{1}{n}, \]

\[ P[X_n=0]=1-\frac{1}{n}. \]

  1. Does \(\{X_n\}\) converge in probability? If so, to what? Why?

  2. Does \(\{X_n\}\) converge in distribution? If so, to what? Why?

  3. Suppose in addition that \(\{X_n\}\) is an independent sequence. Does \(\{X_n\}\) converge almost surely? What is

\[ \limsup_{n\to\infty}X_n \quad\text{and}\quad \liminf_{n\to\infty}X_n \]

almost surely? Explain your answer.

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

(a)

For any fixed \(\epsilon>0\), when \(n>\epsilon\),

\[ P(|X_n|>\epsilon) = P(X_n=n) = \frac{1}{n} \longrightarrow 0. \]

Therefore,

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{P}0. \]

(b)

Let

\[ F_n(x)=P(X_n\leq x). \]

Then

\[ F_n(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\[4pt] 1-\dfrac{1}{n}, & 0\leq x<n,\\[6pt] 1, & x\geq n. \end{cases} \]

For fixed \(x\in\mathbb{R}\), \(n\) will eventually exceed \(x\). Hence,

\[ F_n(x)\longrightarrow F(x):= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ 1, & x\geq 0. \end{cases} \]

This is the distribution function of the constant random variable \(0\). Therefore,

\[ X_n\Rightarrow 0. \]

(c)

Let

\[ A_n=[X_n>0]=[X_n=n]. \]

Since \(\{X_n\}\) is independent, \(\{A_n\}\) is independent, and

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(A_n) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n} = \infty. \]

By the second Borel–Cantelli lemma,

\[ P(A_n\ \text{i.o.})=1. \]

Thus, almost surely, there exists a subsequence \(\{n_k\}\) such that

\[ X_{n_k}=n_k\longrightarrow\infty. \]

Therefore,

\[ \limsup_{n\to\infty}X_n=\infty \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

Now let

\[ B_n=[X_n=0]. \]

Again, \(\{B_n\}\) is independent, and

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{N}P(B_n) = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right) = N-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n} \longrightarrow\infty. \]

Hence,

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(B_n)=\infty. \]

By the second Borel–Cantelli lemma,

\[ P(B_n\ \text{i.o.})=1. \]

Thus \(X_n=0\) infinitely often almost surely. Since \(X_n\geq0\),

\[ \liminf_{n\to\infty}X_n=0 \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

Therefore,

\[ \limsup_{n\to\infty}X_n=\infty, \qquad \liminf_{n\to\infty}X_n=0 \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

Hence \(\{X_n\}\) does not converge almost surely.

Ex 4

  1. Let \(\{X_n, n \ge 0\}\) be positive integer valued random variables. Prove

\[ X_n \Rightarrow X_0 \]

iff for every \(k \ge 0\)

\[ P[X_n=k] \to P[X_0=k]. \]

  1. Let \(\{X_n\}\) be a sequence of random vectors on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) such that \(X_n\) has a discrete distribution having only points with integer components as possible values. Let \(X\) be another such random vector. Show

\[ X_n \Rightarrow X \]

iff

\[ \sum_x \left|P[X_n=x]-P[X=x]\right| \to 0 \]

as \(n\to\infty\). (Use Scheffé’s lemma.)

  1. For events \(\{A_n,n\ge0\}\), prove

\[ 1_{A_n}\Rightarrow 1_{A_0} \quad\text{iff}\quad P(A_n)\to P(A_0). \]

  1. Let \(F_n\) concentrate all mass at \(x_n\) for \(n\ge0\). Prove

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F_0 \quad\text{iff}\quad x_n\to x_0. \]

  1. Let \(X_n=1-1/n\) or \(1+1/n\) each with probability \(1/2\) and suppose \(P[X=1]=1\). Show \(X_n\Rightarrow X\) but that the mass function \(f_n(x)\) of \(X_n\) does not converge for any \(x\).

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

(a)

Let \(F_n\) be the df of \(X_n\).

Suppose first that

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X_0. \]

Since the random variables are integer valued, for every \(k\ge0\) the points

\[ k-\frac12,\qquad k+\frac12 \]

are continuity points of \(F_0\). Hence

\[ F_n\left(k-\frac12\right) \to F_0\left(k-\frac12\right) \]

and

\[ F_n\left(k+\frac12\right) \to F_0\left(k+\frac12\right). \]

But

\[ P(X_n=k) = F_n\left(k+\frac12\right) - F_n\left(k-\frac12\right), \]

so

\[ P(X_n=k)\to P(X_0=k). \]

Conversely, suppose that for every \(k\ge0\),

\[ P(X_n=k)\to P(X_0=k). \]

For any fixed \(x\in\mathbb{R}\),

\[ F_n(x) = \sum_{k\le x}P(X_n=k). \]

Since the random variables are positive integer valued, this is a finite sum for fixed \(x\). Therefore

\[ F_n(x) \to \sum_{k\le x}P(X_0=k) = F_0(x). \]

In particular, this holds at every continuity point of \(F_0\), and hence

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X_0. \]

(b)

Suppose first that

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X. \]

For each

\[ x=(x_1,\ldots,x_d)\in\mathbb{Z}^d, \]

define

\[ B_x = \prod_{i=1}^d \left(x_i-\frac12,x_i+\frac12\right]. \]

Since \(X_n\) and \(X\) can take only values with integer components,

\[ P(X_n\in B_x)=P(X_n=x) \]

and

\[ P(X\in B_x)=P(X=x). \]

The probability of \(B_x\) can be expressed by inclusion-exclusion as a finite alternating sum of the probabilities of \(2^d\) lower orthants. The corners of these lower orthants have coordinates of the form

\[ x_i\pm\frac12. \]

Since \(X\) is integer valued, all these corners are continuity points of its distribution function. Therefore, from \(X_n\Rightarrow X\),

\[ P(X_n\in B_x)\to P(X\in B_x), \]

and hence

\[ P(X_n=x)\to P(X=x) \qquad \text{for every }x\in\mathbb{Z}^d. \]

Now define

\[ p_n(x)=P(X_n=x), \qquad p(x)=P(X=x). \]

These are densities with respect to counting measure on \(\mathbb{Z}^d\), and

\[ \sum_xp_n(x)=\sum_xp(x)=1. \]

Since

\[ p_n(x)\to p(x) \]

pointwise, Scheffé’s lemma applied with respect to counting measure gives

\[ \sum_x|p_n(x)-p(x)|\to0. \]

Therefore

\[ \sum_x \left| P(X_n=x)-P(X=x) \right| \to0. \]

Conversely, suppose

\[ \sum_x \left| P(X_n=x)-P(X=x) \right| \to0. \]

For any \(y\in\mathbb{R}^d\),

\[ \begin{aligned} |F_n(y)-F(y)| &= \left| \sum_{x\le y} \left\{P(X_n=x)-P(X=x)\right\} \right|\\ &\le \sum_x \left| P(X_n=x)-P(X=x) \right| \to0. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(F_n(y)\to F(y)\), in particular at every continuity point of \(F\), and hence

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X. \]

(c)

Let

\[ X_n=1_{A_n}, \qquad X_0=1_{A_0}, \]

and write

\[ p_n=P(A_n), \qquad p_0=P(A_0). \]

Suppose

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X_0. \]

Since \(1/2\) is a continuity point of the df of \(X_0\),

\[ F_n\left(\frac12\right) \to F_0\left(\frac12\right). \]

But

\[ F_n\left(\frac12\right) = P(X_n=0) = 1-p_n, \]

and similarly

\[ F_0\left(\frac12\right)=1-p_0. \]

Therefore

\[ p_n\to p_0, \]

that is,

\[ P(A_n)\to P(A_0). \]

Conversely, suppose

\[ P(A_n)\to P(A_0), \]

or equivalently,

\[ p_n\to p_0. \]

The df of \(X_n\) is

\[ F_n(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ 1-p_n, & 0\le x<1,\\ 1, & x\ge1. \end{cases} \]

Thus \(p_n\to p_0\) implies

\[ F_n(x)\to F_0(x) \]

for every \(x\), and hence

\[ 1_{A_n}\Rightarrow1_{A_0}. \]

(d)

Suppose first that

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F_0. \]

Assume, to the contrary, that

\[ x_n\not\to x_0. \]

Then there exists \(\epsilon_0>0\) and a subsequence \(\{x_{n_k}\}\) such that, after possibly taking another subsequence, either

\[ x_{n_k}\ge x_0+\epsilon_0 \]

for every \(k\), or

\[ x_{n_k}\le x_0-\epsilon_0 \]

for every \(k\).

In the first case, choose

\[ y=x_0+\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}. \]

Then \(y\ne x_0\), so \(y\) is a continuity point of \(F_0\), but

\[ F_{n_k}(y)=0 \]

while

\[ F_0(y)=1, \]

which contradicts \(F_{n_k}(y)\to F_0(y)\).

Similarly, in the second case choose

\[ y=x_0-\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}. \]

Then

\[ F_{n_k}(y)=1 \]

while

\[ F_0(y)=0, \]

again a contradiction.

Therefore

\[ x_n\to x_0. \]

Conversely, suppose

\[ x_n\to x_0. \]

The only discontinuity point of \(F_0\) is \(x_0\).

If \(x<x_0\), then for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ x<x_n, \]

so

\[ F_n(x)=0=F_0(x). \]

If \(x>x_0\), then for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ x>x_n, \]

so

\[ F_n(x)=1=F_0(x). \]

Hence \(F_n(x)\to F_0(x)\) at every continuity point of \(F_0\), and therefore

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F_0. \]

(e)

The df of \(X\) is

\[ F(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x<1,\\ 1, & x\ge1. \end{cases} \]

Thus \(1\) is the only discontinuity point of \(F\).

For any fixed \(x<1\), when \(n\) is sufficiently large,

\[ x<1-\frac1n, \]

and therefore

\[ F_n(x)=0=F(x). \]

For any fixed \(x>1\), when \(n\) is sufficiently large,

\[ x>1+\frac1n, \]

and therefore

\[ F_n(x)=1=F(x). \]

Hence

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X. \]

The mass function of \(X_n\) is

\[ f_n(x) = \begin{cases} \frac12, & x=1-\frac1n,\\ \frac12, & x=1+\frac1n,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \]

while the mass function of \(X\) is

\[ f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x=1,\\ 0, & x\ne1. \end{cases} \]

At \(x=1\),

\[ f_n(1)=0 \]

for every \(n\), whereas

\[ f(1)=1. \]

Thus

\[ f_n(1)\not\to f(1). \]

In fact, for every fixed \(x\),

\[ f_n(x)\to0. \]

Therefore the literal statement in the exercise that \(f_n(x)\) ``does not converge for any \(x\)’’ is not correct: the mass functions converge pointwise to the zero function, but they do not converge pointwise to the mass function \(f\) of the limiting random variable.

Ex 12

Suppose \(F_n\) has density

\[ f_n(x)= \begin{cases} 1-\cos 2n\pi x, & \text{if }0\le x\le 1,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

Show that \(F_n\) converges weakly to the uniform distribution on \([0,1]\) but that the densities \(f_n\) do not converge.

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

For \(0\le x\le 1\),

\[ F_n(x) = \int_0^x(1-\cos 2n\pi t)\,dt = x-\frac{1}{2n\pi}\sin(2n\pi x). \]

Therefore,

\[ F_n(x)\to x. \]

Also, for \(x<0\),

\[ F_n(x)=0, \]

and for \(x>1\),

\[ F_n(x)=1. \]

Hence, if \(F\) is the distribution function of the uniform distribution on \([0,1]\),

\[ F(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ x, & 0\le x\le1,\\ 1, & x>1, \end{cases} \]

then

\[ F_n(x)\to F(x) \]

for every \(x\). Therefore,

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F. \]

However, take the fixed point

\[ x=\frac12. \]

Then

\[ f_n\left(\frac12\right) = 1-\cos(n\pi) = 1-(-1)^n, \]

so

\[ f_n\left(\frac12\right) = \begin{cases} 0, & n\text{ even},\\ 2, & n\text{ odd}. \end{cases} \]

Thus \(f_n(1/2)\) does not converge, and hence the densities \(f_n\) do not converge pointwise.

Ex 18

Give an example of a sequence of discrete distributions that converge weakly to a limit distribution which possesses a density.

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Let \(X_n\) be a random variable that puts mass \(1/n\) uniformly on

\[ \left\{ \frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\ldots,\frac{n}{n} \right\}. \]

\[ P\left(X_n=\frac{k}{n}\right)=\frac1n, \qquad k=1,\ldots,n. \]

\[ F_n(x) = \frac{\lfloor nx\rfloor}{n}. \]

\[ [X_{l,n}\le t] = [N_n(t)\ge l]. \]

\[ P(X_i\le t)=1-e^{-t}, \]

so

\[ N_n(t)\sim \operatorname{Bin}\left(n,1-e^{-t}\right). \]

\[ \begin{aligned} P(nX_{l,n}\le x) &= P\left(X_{l,n}\le\frac{x}{n}\right)\\ &= P\left(N_n\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\ge l\right). \end{aligned} \]

\[ e^{-x(1-k/n)}\to e^{-x}. \]

\[ \begin{aligned} P(nX_{l,n}\le x) &= P\left(N_n\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\ge l\right)\\ &= 1- \sum_{k=0}^{l-1} P\left(N_n\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)=k\right). \end{aligned} \]

where \(Y_0,\ldots,Y_{l-1}\) are iid \(\operatorname{Exp}(1)\).

Therefore,

\[ \sum_{i=0}^{l-1}Y_{i,n} \Rightarrow \sum_{i=0}^{l-1}Y_i. \]

But

\[ \sum_{i=0}^{l-1}Y_i \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(l,1), \]

and

\[ \sum_{i=0}^{l-1}Y_{i,n} = nX_{l,n}. \]

Hence,

\[ nX_{l,n} \Rightarrow \operatorname{Gamma}(l,1). \]

\[ |x_1-x_2|<\delta_0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |F(x_1)-F(x_2)|<\epsilon \]

for all \(x_1,x_2\in[-M-1,M+1]\).

Let

\[ \delta=\min(\delta_0,1). \]

Now take any \(x_1,x_2\in\mathbb{R}\) such that

\[ |x_1-x_2|<\delta. \]

If

\[ x_1,x_2\in[-M-1,M+1], \]

then

\[ |F(x_1)-F(x_2)|<\epsilon. \]

Otherwise, without loss of generality, suppose \(x_1\) is outside this interval.

If

\[ x_1>M+1, \]

then, since \(|x_1-x_2|<1\),

\[ x_2>x_1-1>M. \]

Thus both \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are in the right tail, so

\[ |F(x_1)-F(x_2)|<\epsilon. \]

Similarly, if

\[ x_1<-M-1, \]

then

\[ x_2<x_1+1<-M, \]

so both \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are in the left tail, and again

\[ |F(x_1)-F(x_2)|<\epsilon. \]

Therefore, for every \(\epsilon>0\), there exists \(\delta>0\) such that

\[ |x_1-x_2|<\delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |F(x_1)-F(x_2)|<\epsilon \]

for all \(x_1,x_2\in\mathbb{R}\).

Hence \(F\) is uniformly continuous on \(\mathbb{R}\).

Method 2

Similarly, let \(F\) be a continuous distribution function, and define

\[ a:=F(+\infty), \qquad b:=F(-\infty). \]

Suppose \(F\) is not uniformly continuous. Then there exists \(\epsilon_0>0\) and sequences \(\{x_n\}\), \(\{y_n\}\) such that

\[ |x_n-y_n|<\frac{1}{n}, \]

but

\[ |F(x_n)-F(y_n)|\geq\epsilon_0. \]

Consider the sequence \(\{x_n\}\). By passing to a subsequence if necessary, we must have one of the following:

\[ x_{n_k}\to x\in\mathbb{R}, \]

or

\[ x_{n_k}\to+\infty, \]

or

\[ x_{n_k}\to-\infty. \]

Since

\[ |x_{n_k}-y_{n_k}| < \frac{1}{n_k} \to0, \]

the corresponding sequence \(\{y_{n_k}\}\) has the same limit behavior.

If

\[ x_{n_k}\to x\in\mathbb{R}, \]

then also

\[ y_{n_k}\to x. \]

Since \(F\) is continuous,

\[ F(x_{n_k})\to F(x), \qquad F(y_{n_k})\to F(x), \]

so

\[ |F(x_{n_k})-F(y_{n_k})|\to0. \]

If

\[ x_{n_k}\to+\infty, \]

then also

\[ y_{n_k}\to+\infty, \]

and hence

\[ F(x_{n_k})\to a, \qquad F(y_{n_k})\to a. \]

Therefore,

\[ |F(x_{n_k})-F(y_{n_k})|\to0. \]

Similarly, if

\[ x_{n_k}\to-\infty, \]

then

\[ y_{n_k}\to-\infty, \]

and

\[ F(x_{n_k})\to b, \qquad F(y_{n_k})\to b, \]

so again

\[ |F(x_{n_k})-F(y_{n_k})|\to0. \]

Thus in every case,

\[ |F(x_{n_k})-F(y_{n_k})|\to0, \]

which contradicts

\[ |F(x_{n_k})-F(y_{n_k})|\geq\epsilon_0. \]

Therefore \(F\) is uniformly continuous on \(\mathbb{R}\).

Ex 5

  1. If \(u_n(x)\), \(x\in\mathbb{R}\) are non-decreasing functions for each \(n\) and \(u_n(x)\to u_0(x)\) and \(u_0(\cdot)\) is continuous, then for any \(-\infty<a<b<\infty\)

\[ \sup_{x\in(a,b)}|u_n(x)-u_0(x)|\to0. \]

Thus, convergence of monotone functions to a continuous limit implies local uniform convergence.

  1. Suppose \(F_n\), \(n\geq0\) are proper df’s and \(F_n\Rightarrow F_0\). If \(F_0\) is continuous, show

\[ \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|F_n(x)-F_0(x)|\to0. \]

For instance, in the central limit theorem, where \(F_0\) is the normal distribution, convergence is always uniform.

  1. Give a simple proof of the Glivenko-Cantelli lemma under the additional hypothesis that the underlying distribution is continuous.

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

(a)

Fix \(\epsilon>0\). Divide \([a,b]\) into finitely many pieces

\[ a=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_m=b \]

such that for every \(i\) and every \(x,y\in[x_{i-1},x_i]\),

\[ |u_0(x)-u_0(y)|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}. \]

This is possible since \(u_0\) is uniformly continuous on the compact interval \([a,b]\).

Since

\[ u_n(x_i)\to u_0(x_i) \]

for each endpoint \(x_i\), and there are only finitely many such endpoints, we can choose \(N\) sufficiently large such that, for every \(n\geq N\) and every \(i\),

\[ |u_n(x_i)-u_0(x_i)|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}. \]

Now take any \(x\in[a,b]\). Then \(x\) must fall into some subinterval \([x_{i-1},x_i]\).

Since \(u_n\) is non-decreasing,

\[ u_n(x_{i-1}) \leq u_n(x) \leq u_n(x_i). \]

For the upper bound,

\[ \begin{aligned} u_n(x) &\leq u_n(x_i)\\ &<u_0(x_i)+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\\ &<u_0(x)+\epsilon. \end{aligned} \]

Similarly, for the lower bound,

\[ \begin{aligned} u_n(x) &\geq u_n(x_{i-1})\\ &>u_0(x_{i-1})-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\\ &>u_0(x)-\epsilon. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ |u_n(x)-u_0(x)|<\epsilon. \]

Since \(x\) is arbitrary,

\[ \sup_{x\in[a,b]} |u_n(x)-u_0(x)| <\epsilon \]

for all sufficiently large \(n\). Hence

\[ \sup_{x\in[a,b]} |u_n(x)-u_0(x)| \to0. \]

(b)

Fix \(\epsilon>0\).

Since \(F_0\) is a proper distribution function, we can select \(M>0\) sufficiently large such that

\[ F_0(-M)<\frac{\epsilon}{4} \]

and

\[ 1-F_0(M)<\frac{\epsilon}{4}. \]

Since \(F_0\) is continuous and

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F_0, \]

we have

\[ F_n(-M)\to F_0(-M) \]

and

\[ F_n(M)\to F_0(M). \]

Therefore, for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ |F_n(-M)-F_0(-M)|<\frac{\epsilon}{4} \]

and

\[ |F_n(M)-F_0(M)|<\frac{\epsilon}{4}. \]

On the compact interval \([-M,M]\), by part (a),

\[ \sup_{x\in[-M,M]} |F_n(x)-F_0(x)| \to0. \]

Now consider the right tail. For \(x\geq M\), by monotonicity,

\[ F_n(M)\leq F_n(x)\leq1 \]

and

\[ F_0(M)\leq F_0(x)\leq1. \]

Thus

\[ 1-F_n(x)\leq1-F_n(M), \]

and

\[ 1-F_0(x)\leq1-F_0(M). \]

Also,

\[ \begin{aligned} 1-F_n(M) &\leq 1-F_0(M)+|F_n(M)-F_0(M)|\\ &< \frac{\epsilon}{2}. \end{aligned} \]

Hence, for every \(x\geq M\),

\[ \begin{aligned} |F_n(x)-F_0(x)| &\leq |1-F_n(x)|+|1-F_0(x)|\\ &\leq 1-F_n(M)+1-F_0(M)\\ &< \epsilon. \end{aligned} \]

Similarly, for the left tail \(x\leq-M\),

\[ 0\leq F_n(x)\leq F_n(-M) \]

and

\[ 0\leq F_0(x)\leq F_0(-M). \]

Moreover,

\[ \begin{aligned} F_n(-M) &\leq F_0(-M)+|F_n(-M)-F_0(-M)|\\ &< \frac{\epsilon}{2}. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, for every \(x\leq-M\),

\[ \begin{aligned} |F_n(x)-F_0(x)| &\leq F_n(x)+F_0(x)\\ &\leq F_n(-M)+F_0(-M)\\ &< \epsilon. \end{aligned} \]

Combining the middle interval and the two tails,

\[ \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}} |F_n(x)-F_0(x)| \to0. \]

(c)

Let

\[ \widehat F_n(x,\omega) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n 1_{\{X_i\leq x\}}(\omega) \]

be the empirical distribution function, where \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) are iid with continuous distribution function \(F\).

For each fixed \(x\),

\[ E\left(1_{\{X_i\leq x\}}\right) = F(x). \]

Therefore, by the SLLN,

\[ \widehat F_n(x,\omega)\to F(x) \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

However, for each fixed \(x\), this means that there exists a set \(A_x\) with

\[ P(A_x)=1 \]

such that, for every \(\omega\in A_x\),

\[ \widehat F_n(x,\omega)\to F(x). \]

The set \(A_x\) may depend on \(x\), and there are uncountably many \(x\in\mathbb{R}\), so we cannot directly take an uncountable intersection.

Instead, use the countable dense set \(\mathbb{Q}\).

For every \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\), let

\[ A_q = \left\{ \omega: \widehat F_n(q,\omega)\to F(q) \right\}. \]

Then

\[ P(A_q)=1. \]

Define

\[ A = \bigcap_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}A_q. \]

Since \(\mathbb{Q}\) is countable,

\[ P(A)=1. \]

Fix \(\omega\in A\).

Then

\[ \widehat F_n(q,\omega)\to F(q) \]

for every \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\).

Now take any \(x\in\mathbb{R}\). Choose \(q_1,q_2\in\mathbb{Q}\) such that

\[ q_1<x<q_2. \]

Since every \(\widehat F_n(\cdot,\omega)\) is non-decreasing,

\[ \widehat F_n(q_1,\omega) \leq \widehat F_n(x,\omega) \leq \widehat F_n(q_2,\omega). \]

Taking limits,

\[ F(q_1) \leq \liminf_{n\to\infty}\widehat F_n(x,\omega) \leq \limsup_{n\to\infty}\widehat F_n(x,\omega) \leq F(q_2). \]

Now let

\[ q_1\uparrow x \]

and

\[ q_2\downarrow x. \]

Since \(F\) is continuous,

\[ F(q_1)\to F(x) \]

and

\[ F(q_2)\to F(x). \]

Therefore, by squeezing,

\[ \widehat F_n(x,\omega)\to F(x). \]

Since \(x\) was arbitrary, for every \(\omega\in A\),

\[ \widehat F_n(x,\omega)\to F(x) \qquad \text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}. \]

Since \(F\) is continuous, part (b) now gives, for every \(\omega\in A\),

\[ \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}} |\widehat F_n(x,\omega)-F(x)| \to0. \]

Since

\[ P(A)=1, \]

we conclude

\[ \boxed{ \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}} |\widehat F_n(x)-F(x)| \to0 \qquad\text{a.s.} } \]

Ex 9

Let \(\{X_n,n\geq0\}\) be random variables. Show \(X_n\Rightarrow X_0\) iff

\[ E(g(X_n))\to E(g(X_0)) \]

for all continuous functions \(g\) with compact support.

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Let \(C_c(\mathbb{R})\) be the set of all continuous functions with compact support on \(\mathbb{R}\), and let \(C_b(\mathbb{R})\) be the set of all bounded continuous functions.

\(\Rightarrow\)

Since

\[ C_c(\mathbb{R})\subset C_b(\mathbb{R}), \]

if

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X_0, \]

then by the Portmanteau theorem,

\[ E(g(X_n))\to E(g(X_0)) \]

for every \(g\in C_c(\mathbb{R})\).

\(\Leftarrow\)

We should notice that in the proof of the Portmanteau theorem \((2)\Rightarrow(1)\), Resnick did not actually use all bounded continuous functions. Instead, the functions used in the proof are continuous functions with compact support.

Let \(F_n\) be the distribution function of \(X_n\), \(n\geq0\), and let

\[ a,b\in C(F_0), \qquad a<b. \]

Firstly, define \(g_k\) whose graph is the trapezoid of height \(1\) obtained by taking a rectangle of height \(1\) with base \([a,b]\) and extending the base symmetrically to

\[ \left[a-\frac1k,b+\frac1k\right]. \]

Then

\[ g_k\in C_c(\mathbb{R}), \]

and

\[ g_k\downarrow 1_{[a,b]}. \]

Also,

\[ 1_{(a,b]}\leq g_k. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} F_n((a,b]) &= \int_{\mathbb{R}}1_{(a,b]}\,dF_n\\ &\leq \int_{\mathbb{R}}g_k\,dF_n\\ &= E(g_k(X_n)). \end{aligned} \]

For fixed \(k\), by assumption,

\[ E(g_k(X_n)) \to E(g_k(X_0)) = \int_{\mathbb{R}}g_k\,dF_0. \]

Hence

\[ \limsup_{n\to\infty}F_n((a,b]) \leq \int_{\mathbb{R}}g_k\,dF_0. \]

Since \(0\leq g_k\leq1\) and

\[ g_k\downarrow1_{[a,b]}, \]

by DCT,

\[ \int_{\mathbb{R}}g_k\,dF_0 \to F_0([a,b]). \]

Since \(a\in C(F_0)\),

\[ F_0([a,b])=F_0((a,b]). \]

Thus

\[ \limsup_{n\to\infty}F_n((a,b]) \leq F_0((a,b]). \]

Secondly, define \(h_k\) whose graph is the trapezoid of height \(1\) obtained by taking a rectangle of height \(1\) with base

\[ \left[a+\frac1k,b-\frac1k\right] \]

and extending the base symmetrically to \([a,b]\), for \(k\) sufficiently large.

Then

\[ h_k\in C_c(\mathbb{R}), \]

and

\[ h_k\uparrow1_{(a,b)}. \]

Also,

\[ h_k\leq1_{(a,b]}. \]

Therefore,

\[ F_n((a,b]) \geq \int_{\mathbb{R}}h_k\,dF_n = E(h_k(X_n)). \]

For fixed \(k\),

\[ E(h_k(X_n)) \to E(h_k(X_0)) = \int_{\mathbb{R}}h_k\,dF_0. \]

Hence

\[ \liminf_{n\to\infty}F_n((a,b]) \geq \int_{\mathbb{R}}h_k\,dF_0. \]

Since \(0\leq h_k\leq1\) and

\[ h_k\uparrow1_{(a,b)}, \]

by DCT,

\[ \int_{\mathbb{R}}h_k\,dF_0 \to F_0((a,b)). \]

Since \(a,b\in C(F_0)\),

\[ F_0((a,b)) = F_0((a,b]). \]

Therefore,

\[ \liminf_{n\to\infty}F_n((a,b]) \geq F_0((a,b]). \]

Combining the two inequalities,

\[ F_n((a,b]) \to F_0((a,b]) \]

for every finite interval of continuity \((a,b]\) of \(F_0\).

Thus

\[ F_n\to F_0 \]

vaguely. Since \(\{F_n,n\geq0\}\) are proper distributions, by Theorem 8.1.1,

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F_0. \]

Therefore,

\[ X_n\Rightarrow X_0. \]

\(\textbf{Note:}\)

There is another possible way to prove the converse without using Theorem 8.1.1, but it is much more complicated.

Suppose

\[ E(g(X_n))\to E(g(X_0)) \]

for every \(g\in C_c(\mathbb R)\). To use the Portmanteau theorem directly, we would need to extend this result to every bounded continuous function

\[ f\in C_b(\mathbb R). \]

For a bounded continuous \(f\), define a continuous cutoff \(h_m\) such that

\[ 0\le h_m\le1, \]

\[ h_m=1\quad\text{on }[-m,m], \]

and

\[ h_m=0\quad\text{outside }[-m-1,m+1]. \]

Then

\[ g_m:=fh_m\in C_c(\mathbb R), \]

so for every fixed \(m\),

\[ E(g_m(X_n))\to E(g_m(X_0)). \]

Also,

\[ g_m(x)\to f(x) \]

pointwise as \(m\to\infty\).

However, this is not enough to immediately conclude

\[ E(f(X_n))\to E(f(X_0)), \]

because there are now two limits,

\[ n\to\infty \qquad\text{and}\qquad m\to\infty, \]

and DCT only gives

\[ E(g_m(X_n))\to E(f(X_n)) \]

for each fixed \(n\). We still need some control that is uniform in \(n\).

To obtain this, we again use the cutoff \(h_m\). Since \(h_m\in C_c(\mathbb R)\),

\[ E(h_m(X_n))\to E(h_m(X_0)) \]

for fixed \(m\), while

\[ E(h_m(X_0))\to1 \]

as \(m\to\infty\). Therefore, for large \(m\) and then large \(n\),

\[ E(1-h_m(X_n)) \]

is small. If \(\|f\|_\infty\le C\), then

\[ \begin{aligned} |E(f(X_n))-E(f(X_0))| &\le |E(f(X_n)-g_m(X_n))|\\ &\quad+ |E(g_m(X_n))-E(g_m(X_0))|\\ &\quad+ |E(g_m(X_0)-f(X_0))|, \end{aligned} \]

and the first and third terms are controlled by

\[ C\,E(1-h_m(X_n)) \]

and

\[ C\,E(1-h_m(X_0)), \]

while the middle term converges to \(0\) by the assumption on \(C_c(\mathbb R)\).

Hence we can eventually prove

\[ E(f(X_n))\to E(f(X_0)) \]

for every \(f\in C_b(\mathbb R)\), and then apply the Portmanteau theorem.

So this longer route is

\[ C_c \longrightarrow \text{tail control} \longrightarrow C_b \longrightarrow \text{Portmanteau} \longrightarrow X_n\Rightarrow X_0. \]

By contrast, using Theorem 8.1.1, the proof becomes

\[ C_c \longrightarrow \text{convergence on finite continuity intervals} \longrightarrow \text{vague convergence} \overset{\text{properness}}{\Longrightarrow} \text{weak convergence}. \]

\[ \boxed{ \text{Theorem 8.1.1 lets us stop at convergence on continuity intervals; } \text{without it, we have to build the whole }C_c\to C_b\text{ bridge.} } \]

Ex 36

Suppose \(F_n\Rightarrow F_0\) and \(g\geq 0\) is continuous and satisfies

\[ \int_{\mathbb{R}} g\,dF_n=1 \]

for all \(n\geq 0\). Define the new measure

\[ G_n(A)=\int_A g\,dF_n. \]

Show \(G_n\Rightarrow G_0\).

(You can either do this directly or use Scheffe’s lemma.)

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Since

\[ g\geq0 \]

and

\[ \int_{\mathbb{R}}g\,dF_n=1, \]

\(\{G_n,n\geq0\}\) are proper probability distributions.

We can use the result from Ex 9. To prove

\[ G_n\Rightarrow G_0, \]

it suffices to show that for every \(h\in C_c(\mathbb{R})\),

\[ \int_{\mathbb{R}}h\,dG_n \to \int_{\mathbb{R}}h\,dG_0. \]

By the definition of \(G_n\),

\[ \int_{\mathbb{R}}h\,dG_n = \int_{\mathbb{R}}hg\,dF_n. \]

Note that \(h\in C_c(\mathbb{R})\) and \(g\) is continuous, so \(hg\) is continuous. Also,

\[ \operatorname{supp}(hg) \subseteq \operatorname{supp}(h), \]

and \(\operatorname{supp}(h)\) is compact. Therefore,

\[ hg\in C_c(\mathbb{R}). \]

Since

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F_0, \]

by Ex 9,

\[ \int_{\mathbb{R}}hg\,dF_n \to \int_{\mathbb{R}}hg\,dF_0. \]

Thus,

\[ \int_{\mathbb{R}}h\,dG_n \to \int_{\mathbb{R}}h\,dG_0 \]

for every \(h\in C_c(\mathbb{R})\).

Using Ex 9 again,

\[ G_n\Rightarrow G_0. \]

\(\textbf{Note:}\)

There are two other possible routes, but both are more complicated than using Ex 9 directly.

The first route is to truncate \(g\) by

\[ g_M=g\wedge M. \]

Then \(g_M\) is bounded and continuous, so Portmanteau can be applied to \(g_M\) or to products involving \(g_M\). The remaining truncation error is controlled using

\[ \int g\,dF_n=1. \]

This is similar to the longer route we originally considered in Ex 9: first extend a result for a smaller class of test functions to a larger class, and then apply Portmanteau.

The second route follows the Scheffe hint. By Baby Skorohod, construct

\[ X_n^\#\overset{d}{=}F_n \]

on \(([0,1],\lambda)\) such that

\[ X_n^\#\to X_0^\# \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

Then

\[ g(X_n^\#)\to g(X_0^\#) \qquad\text{a.s.}, \]

and

\[ \int_0^1 g(X_n^\#)\,d\lambda = \int_{\mathbb{R}}g\,dF_n = 1. \]

Thus \(\{g(X_n^\#)\}\) are densities with respect to the same measure \(\lambda\), and Scheffe’s lemma gives

\[ \int_0^1 \left| g(X_n^\#)-g(X_0^\#) \right| \,d\lambda \to0. \]

One then introduces another bounded continuous test function to convert this \(L^1\) convergence of the weights into weak convergence of \(G_n\).

Compared with these two routes, Ex 9 makes the proof much shorter: for \(h\in C_c(\mathbb{R})\),

\[ hg\in C_c(\mathbb{R}) \]

automatically, even though \(g\) itself may not be bounded.

Since \(Y_n\overset{d}{=}X_n\) and \(Y_0\overset{d}{=}X_0\),

\[ E(X_n)\to E(X_0), \]

and

\[ E(X_n^2)\to E(X_0^2). \]

Finally,

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X_n) &= E(X_n^2)-[E(X_n)]^2\\ &\to E(X_0^2)-[E(X_0)]^2\\ &= \operatorname{Var}(X_0). \end{aligned} \]

Hence,

\[ E(X_n)\to E(X_0) \]

and

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X_n)\to\operatorname{Var}(X_0). \]

\[ f_n(x) = c_n\exp\left(-\frac{\|x\|^2}{2}\right), \]

which depends only on \(\|x\|\).

\[ QN^{(n)}\overset{d}{=}N^{(n)}. \]

Ex 24

Show that the normal type is closed under convolution. In other words, if \(N_1,N_2\) are two independent normally distributed random variables, show that \(N_1+N_2\) is also normally distributed. Prove a similar statement for the Poisson and Cauchy types.

Is a similar statement true for the exponential type?

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

For this study record, we work out the normal and exponential cases. We do not repeat the Poisson and Cauchy calculations here.

Normal type

Let

\[ N_1\sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2), \qquad N_2\sim N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2), \]

and suppose \(N_1\) and \(N_2\) are independent.

Set

\[ Z=N_1+N_2, \qquad U=N_2. \]

Then

\[ N_1=Z-U, \qquad N_2=U. \]

The transformation

\[ (n_1,n_2)\mapsto(z,u) = (n_1+n_2,n_2) \]

has inverse

\[ (z,u)\mapsto(z-u,u). \]

The Jacobian of the inverse transformation is

\[ \left| \frac{\partial(n_1,n_2)} {\partial(z,u)} \right| = \left| \begin{matrix} 1 & -1\\ 0 & 1 \end{matrix} \right| = 1. \]

Since \(N_1\) and \(N_2\) are independent, their joint density is

\[ f_{N_1,N_2}(n_1,n_2) = f_1(n_1)f_2(n_2). \]

Therefore,

\[ f_{Z,U}(z,u) = f_1(z-u)f_2(u), \]

and hence the marginal density of \(Z\) is

\[ f_Z(z) = \int_{\mathbb R} f_1(z-u)f_2(u)\,du. \]

For the normal densities,

\[ f_1(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_1} \exp\left\{ -\frac{(x-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2} \right\}, \]

and

\[ f_2(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_2} \exp\left\{ -\frac{(x-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2} \right\}. \]

Thus,

\[ f_Z(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2} \int_{\mathbb R} \exp\left\{ -\frac12 \left[ \frac{(z-u-\mu_1)^2}{\sigma_1^2} + \frac{(u-\mu_2)^2}{\sigma_2^2} \right] \right\} du. \]

Let

\[ A = \frac1{\sigma_1^2} + \frac1{\sigma_2^2} = \frac{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2} {\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2}, \]

and

\[ m(z) = \frac{ \sigma_2^2(z-\mu_1)+\sigma_1^2\mu_2 }{ \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2 }. \]

Completing the square,

\[ \frac{(z-u-\mu_1)^2}{\sigma_1^2} + \frac{(u-\mu_2)^2}{\sigma_2^2} = A(u-m(z))^2 + \frac{(z-\mu_1-\mu_2)^2} {\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} f_Z(z) &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2} \exp\left\{ -\frac{(z-\mu_1-\mu_2)^2} {2(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)} \right\}\\ &\qquad\times \int_{\mathbb R} \exp\left\{ -\frac A2(u-m(z))^2 \right\} du. \end{aligned} \]

Since

\[ \int_{\mathbb R} \exp\left\{ -\frac A2(u-m(z))^2 \right\} du = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{A}}, \]

we get

\[ f_Z(z) = \frac{1} {\sqrt{2\pi(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)}} \exp\left\{ -\frac{(z-\mu_1-\mu_2)^2} {2(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)} \right\}. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ N_1+N_2 \sim N(\mu_1+\mu_2,\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2). } \]

Therefore the normal type is closed under convolution.

Exponential type

Let

\[ X_1\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda_1), \qquad X_2\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda_2), \]

independently, and set

\[ Z=X_1+X_2. \]

By the same convolution formula,

\[ f_Z(x) = \int_{\mathbb R} f_1(x-u)f_2(u)\,du. \]

We have

\[ f_1(x-u) = \lambda_1e^{-\lambda_1(x-u)} 1_{\{x-u>0\}}, \]

and

\[ f_2(u) = \lambda_2e^{-\lambda_2u} 1_{\{u>0\}}. \]

Thus the two indicators require

\[ 0<u<x. \]

Therefore, for \(x>0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} f_Z(x) &= \int_0^x \lambda_1\lambda_2 e^{-\lambda_1(x-u)} e^{-\lambda_2u}\,du\\ &= \lambda_1\lambda_2e^{-\lambda_1x} \int_0^x e^{-(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)u}\,du. \end{aligned} \]

If

\[ \lambda_1=\lambda_2=\lambda, \]

then

\[ f_Z(x) = \lambda^2xe^{-\lambda x}, \qquad x>0. \]

This is a Gamma\((2,\lambda)\) density, not an exponential density.

If

\[ \lambda_1\neq\lambda_2, \]

then

\[ \begin{aligned} f_Z(x) &= \lambda_1\lambda_2e^{-\lambda_1x} \frac{ 1-e^{-(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)x} }{ \lambda_2-\lambda_1 }\\ &= \frac{\lambda_1\lambda_2} {\lambda_2-\lambda_1} \left( e^{-\lambda_1x} - e^{-\lambda_2x} \right), \qquad x>0. \end{aligned} \]

Also,

\[ f_Z(x)=0, \qquad x\leq0. \]

Again, this is not an exponential density.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{the exponential type is not closed under convolution}. } \]

In fact, the equal-rate case alone already gives the counterexample

\[ \operatorname{Exp}(\lambda) * \operatorname{Exp}(\lambda) = \operatorname{Gamma}(2,\lambda). \]

\(\textbf{Note: Differentiating the convolution CDF}\)

Instead of using the Jacobian method, we could begin from

\[ F_Z(z) = \int_{\mathbb R} F_1(z-u)f_2(u)\,du. \]

To justify differentiating under the integral sign, consider the difference quotient

\[ \frac{F_Z(z+h)-F_Z(z)}{h} = \int_{\mathbb R} \frac{ F_1(z+h-u)-F_1(z-u) }{ h } f_2(u)\,du. \]

For every fixed \(u\),

\[ \frac{ F_1(z+h-u)-F_1(z-u) }{ h } \to f_1(z-u) \]

as \(h\to0\).

For the normal density, by the mean value theorem,

\[ \left| \frac{ F_1(z+h-u)-F_1(z-u) }{ h } \right| \leq \sup_{x\in\mathbb R}f_1(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_1}. \]

Hence,

\[ \left| \frac{ F_1(z+h-u)-F_1(z-u) }{ h } f_2(u) \right| \leq \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_1}f_2(u). \]

Since

\[ \int_{\mathbb R}f_2(u)\,du=1, \]

the right-hand side is integrable. Therefore DCT allows the limit to pass through the integral:

\[ \begin{aligned} F_Z'(z) &= \int_{\mathbb R} f_1(z-u)f_2(u)\,du. \end{aligned} \]

Thus we recover the same convolution density.

\(\textbf{Note: Classical uniform convergence argument}\)

In basic probability or calculus, the same interchange may instead be justified using a classical differentiation-under-the-integral theorem.

For the normal density,

\[ f_1(z-u)f_2(u) \leq \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_1}f_2(u) \]

uniformly in \(z\).

Therefore,

\[ \sup_{z\in\mathbb R} \int_{|u|>M} f_1(z-u)f_2(u)\,du \leq \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_1} \int_{|u|>M}f_2(u)\,du \to0. \]

Hence the improper integral

\[ \int_{\mathbb R} f_1(z-u)f_2(u)\,du \]

converges uniformly in \(z\), which is the classical condition allowing differentiation and integration to be interchanged.

Thus the older uniform-convergence argument and the present DCT argument are two versions of the same underlying control.

\(\textbf{Why I did this calculation:}\)

The process in this exercise is almost purely calculus. I did the normal convolution calculation explicitly to illustrate how tedious it would be to work with sums of independent random variables without the concept of characteristic functions introduced in Chapter 9.

Once this calculation has been done once, repeating the same type of algebra for the Poisson and Cauchy cases does not add much to this particular purpose. The exponential case is still worth checking because it answers a different structural question: unlike the normal type, the exponential type is not closed under convolution.

Ex 6

Let \(F\) be a non-degenerate df and suppose for \(a>0\), \(c>0\) and \(b\in\mathbb{R}\), \(d\in\mathbb{R}\), that for all \(x\)

\[ F(ax+b)=F(cx+d). \]

Prove that \(a=c\) and \(b=d\). Do this 2 ways:

  1. Considering inverse functions.

  2. Showing it is enough to prove \(F(Ax+B)=F(x)\) for all \(x\) implies \(A=1\), and \(B=17\) (just kidding, \(B=0\)). If \(Tx=Ax+B\) then iterate the relation \(F(Tx)=F(x)\) again and again.

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

(i)

Let

\[ G(x)=F(ax+b) \]

and

\[ H(x)=F(cx+d). \]

By assumption,

\[ G(x)=H(x) \]

for every \(x\in\mathbb{R}\), so

\[ G^{\leftarrow}(y)=H^{\leftarrow}(y). \]

By definition,

\[ \begin{aligned} G^{\leftarrow}(y) &= \inf\{x:G(x)\geq y\}\\ &= \inf\{x:F(ax+b)\geq y\}. \end{aligned} \]

Let

\[ u=ax+b. \]

Since \(a>0\),

\[ x=\frac{u-b}{a}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} G^{\leftarrow}(y) &= \inf \left\{ \frac{u-b}{a}:F(u)\geq y \right\}\\ &= \frac{F^{\leftarrow}(y)-b}{a}. \end{aligned} \]

Similarly,

\[ H^{\leftarrow}(y) = \frac{F^{\leftarrow}(y)-d}{c}. \]

Hence,

\[ \frac{F^{\leftarrow}(y)-b}{a} = \frac{F^{\leftarrow}(y)-d}{c}. \]

Since \(F\) is non-degenerate, \(F^{\leftarrow}\) is not constant on \((0,1)\). Therefore, we can select \(y_1<y_2\) such that

\[ F^{\leftarrow}(y_1) \neq F^{\leftarrow}(y_2). \]

Then

\[ \frac{F^{\leftarrow}(y_1)-b}{a} = \frac{F^{\leftarrow}(y_1)-d}{c} \]

and

\[ \frac{F^{\leftarrow}(y_2)-b}{a} = \frac{F^{\leftarrow}(y_2)-d}{c}. \]

Subtracting,

\[ \frac{ F^{\leftarrow}(y_1)-F^{\leftarrow}(y_2) }{a} = \frac{ F^{\leftarrow}(y_1)-F^{\leftarrow}(y_2) }{c}. \]

Since

\[ F^{\leftarrow}(y_1)-F^{\leftarrow}(y_2)\neq0, \]

we have

\[ a=c. \]

Substituting this back into either equality gives

\[ b=d. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{a=c,\qquad b=d.} \]

(ii)

First, we show that it is enough to prove that

\[ F(Ax+B)=F(x), \qquad A>0, \]

for every \(x\), implies

\[ A=1,\qquad B=0. \]

Starting from

\[ F(ax+b)=F(cx+d), \]

let

\[ y=ax+b. \]

Then

\[ x=\frac{y-b}{a}, \]

so

\[ \begin{aligned} F(y) &= F\left( c\frac{y-b}{a}+d \right)\\ &= F\left( \frac ca y+d-\frac{cb}{a} \right). \end{aligned} \]

Thus, if we set

\[ A=\frac ca>0 \]

and

\[ B=d-\frac{cb}{a}, \]

then

\[ F(Ay+B)=F(y) \]

for every \(y\in\mathbb{R}\).

So it is enough to prove that

\[ F(Ax+B)=F(x) \]

for every \(x\) implies

\[ A=1,\qquad B=0. \]

Let

\[ T(x)=Ax+B. \]

Then

\[ F(Tx)=F(x). \]

Therefore, by iteration,

\[ F(T^n x)=F(x) \]

for every \(n\geq1\).

First, we find the form of \(T^n(x)\).

We have

\[ \begin{aligned} T^2(x) &= T(Tx)\\ &= A(Ax+B)+B\\ &= A^2x+AB+B, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} T^3(x) &= T(T^2x)\\ &= A(A^2x+AB+B)+B\\ &= A^3x+A^2B+AB+B. \end{aligned} \]

This suggests

\[ T^n(x) = A^n x+ \left( \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}A^j \right)B. \]

We prove this by induction.

Suppose

\[ T^n(x) = A^n x+ \left( \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}A^j \right)B. \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} T^{n+1}(x) &= T(T^n(x))\\ &= A\left[ A^n x+ \left( \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}A^j \right)B \right]+B\\ &= A^{n+1}x+ \left( \sum_{j=1}^{n}A^j \right)B+B\\ &= A^{n+1}x+ \left( \sum_{j=0}^{n}A^j \right)B. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, by induction,

\[ \boxed{ T^n(x) = A^n x+ \left( \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}A^j \right)B. } \]

Now suppose

\[ A\neq1. \]

We solve for the fixed point \(x_0\) of \(T\):

\[ T(x_0)=x_0. \]

That is,

\[ Ax_0+B=x_0, \]

so

\[ x_0=\frac{B}{1-A}. \]

Since

\[ T(x_0)=x_0, \]

we have

\[ T^n(x_0)=x_0 \]

for every \(n\).

Using the iteration formula,

\[ T^n(x)-T^n(x_0) = A^n(x-x_0). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ T^n(x)-x_0 = A^n(x-x_0) } \]

or equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ T^n(x) = x_0+A^n(x-x_0). } \]

This form makes the behavior of the iterates clear.

Case 1: \(A>1\)

If

\[ x>x_0, \]

then

\[ T^n(x)\to+\infty. \]

Since

\[ F(x)=F(T^n(x)) \]

for every \(n\),

\[ F(x)=1 \]

for every \(x>x_0\).

If

\[ x<x_0, \]

then

\[ T^n(x)\to-\infty. \]

Therefore,

\[ F(x)=0 \]

for every \(x<x_0\).

By right continuity,

\[ F(x_0)=1. \]

Hence

\[ F(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x<x_0,\\ 1, & x\geq x_0, \end{cases} \]

so \(F\) concentrates all its mass at \(x_0\).

This contradicts the assumption that \(F\) is non-degenerate.

Therefore,

\[ A>1 \]

is impossible.

Case 2: \(0<A<1\)

In this case,

\[ T^n(x)\to x_0. \]

If

\[ x>x_0, \]

then

\[ T^n(x)\downarrow x_0. \]

Since \(F\) is right-continuous,

\[ F(x) = F(T^n(x)) \to F(x_0). \]

Thus

\[ F(x)=F(x_0) \]

for every \(x>x_0\).

Letting \(x\to+\infty\) gives

\[ F(x_0)=1. \]

If

\[ x<x_0, \]

then

\[ T^n(x)\uparrow x_0. \]

Therefore,

\[ F(x) = F(T^n(x)) \to F(x_0-). \]

Thus

\[ F(x)=F(x_0-) \]

for every \(x<x_0\).

Letting \(x\to-\infty\) gives

\[ F(x_0-)=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ F(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x<x_0,\\ 1, & x\geq x_0. \end{cases} \]

Again, \(F\) concentrates all its mass at \(x_0\), contradicting non-degeneracy.

Therefore,

\[ 0<A<1 \]

is also impossible.

Hence,

\[ A=1. \]

In this case,

\[ T(x)=x+B. \]

From the iteration formula,

\[ T^n(x)=x+nB. \]

If \(B>0\), then

\[ T^n(x)\to+\infty. \]

\[ \boxed{ \frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}\to1. } \]

\[ \boxed{ a_n\to\infty, \qquad \frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}\to1. } \]

Ex 17

If \(\{X_n\}\) are iid \(U(0,1)\) random variables, find a non-degenerate limit distribution for

\[ M_n=\bigvee_{i=1}^n X_i \]

under suitable normalization.

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Let

\[ M_n=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_i, \]

where \(\{X_i\}\) are iid \(U(0,1)\) random variables.

For \(0\leq x\leq1\),

\[ \begin{aligned} P(M_n\leq x) &= P(X_1\leq x,\ldots,X_n\leq x)\\ &= x^n. \end{aligned} \]

First, without normalization,

\[ M_n\Rightarrow 1, \]

whose df is

\[ F(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x<1,\\ 1, & x\geq1. \end{cases} \]

This limit is degenerate. Therefore, we need to examine the fluctuation of \(M_n\) around the endpoint \(1\).

Try

\[ x=1-\frac{y}{n}, \qquad y\geq0. \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} P\left(M_n\leq1-\frac{y}{n}\right) &= \left(1-\frac{y}{n}\right)^n\\ &\longrightarrow e^{-y}. \end{aligned} \]

But

\[ M_n\leq1-\frac{y}{n} \]

is equivalent to

\[ n(1-M_n)\geq y. \]

Therefore,

\[ P\left(n(1-M_n)\geq y\right) \longrightarrow e^{-y}. \]

Hence,

\[ P\left(n(1-M_n)\leq y\right) \longrightarrow 1-e^{-y}, \qquad y\geq0. \]

For \(y<0\),

\[ P\left(n(1-M_n)\leq y\right)=0, \]

since \(M_n\leq1\).

Thus

\[ \boxed{ n(1-M_n)\Rightarrow E, \qquad E\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1). } \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ n(M_n-1)\Rightarrow -E. } \]

Connection with Section 8.7.1

Section 8.7.1 considers non-degenerate limits of centered and scaled maxima of the form

\[ \frac{M_n-b_n}{a_n}\Rightarrow G, \qquad a_n>0. \]

In this exercise, take

\[ b_n=1, \qquad a_n=\frac1n. \]

Then

\[ \frac{M_n-b_n}{a_n} = n(M_n-1). \]

Let

\[ Y=-E, \qquad E\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1). \]

The df of \(Y\) is, for \(x<0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} P(Y\leq x) &= P(-E\leq x)\\ &= P(E\geq -x)\\ &= e^x, \end{aligned} \]

while for \(x\geq0\),

\[ P(Y\leq x)=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ G(x) = \begin{cases} e^x, & x<0,\\ 1, & x\geq0. \end{cases} \]

But the Weibull extreme-value distribution in Section 8.7.1 is

\[ \Psi_\alpha(x) = \begin{cases} \exp\{-(-x)^\alpha\}, & x<0,\\ 1, & x\geq0, \end{cases} \qquad \alpha>0. \]

Taking \(\alpha=1\) gives

\[ \Psi_1(x) = \begin{cases} e^x, & x<0,\\ 1, & x\geq0. \end{cases} \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ n(M_n-1) = \frac{M_n-1}{1/n} \Rightarrow \Psi_1. } \]

So the exponential formulation

\[ n(1-M_n)\Rightarrow\operatorname{Exp}(1) \]

and the extreme-value formulation

\[ n(M_n-1)\Rightarrow\Psi_1 \]

are the same limit theorem written with opposite signs.

\(\textbf{Note:}\)

My first observation was that \(M_n\) itself converges to the degenerate distribution concentrated at the right endpoint \(1\). This identifies the location but does not yet give a non-degenerate limit.

The next step is therefore to determine the correct local scale around \(1\). Choosing

\[ 1-\frac{y}{n} \]

gives

\[ \left(1-\frac{y}{n}\right)^n\to e^{-y}, \]

showing that the fluctuation scale is \(1/n\).

This also gives a concrete example of Section 8.7.1:

\[ U(0,1) \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{finite right endpoint }1 \quad\longrightarrow\quad b_n=1,\ a_n=\frac1n \quad\longrightarrow\quad \Psi_1\text{ (Weibull type)}. \]

This example also provides a useful sanity check for the sign classification in the proof of the extreme-value theorem: a distribution with a finite upper endpoint produces a Weibull-type limit.

Ex 27

Suppose \(\{X_n,n\geq1\}\) are iid and non-negative random variables with common density \(f(x)\) satisfying

\[ \lambda:=\lim_{t\downarrow0}f(t)>0. \]

Show

\[ n\bigwedge_{i=1}^n X_i \]

has a limit distribution. (This is extreme value theory, but for minima not maxima.)

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Let

\[ Y_n:=n\min_{1\leq i\leq n}X_i. \]

Since the \(X_i\) are iid, for \(x>0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} P(Y_n>x) &= P\left( n\min_{1\leq i\leq n}X_i>x \right)\\ &= P\left( \min_{1\leq i\leq n}X_i>\frac{x}{n} \right)\\ &= \prod_{i=1}^n P\left(X_i>\frac{x}{n}\right)\\ &= \left( 1-F\left(\frac{x}{n}\right) \right)^n. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}P(Y_n>x) &= n \left( 1-F\left(\frac{x}{n}\right) \right)^{n-1} \left( -f\left(\frac{x}{n}\right) \right) \frac1n\\ &= - f\left(\frac{x}{n}\right) \left( 1-F\left(\frac{x}{n}\right) \right)^{n-1}. \end{aligned} \]

Hence the density of \(Y_n\), denoted by \(g_n\), is

\[ g_n(x) = f\left(\frac{x}{n}\right) \left( 1-F\left(\frac{x}{n}\right) \right)^{n-1}, \qquad x>0. \]

Since the \(X_i\) are non-negative and have density \(f\),

\[ F(0)=0. \]

Also,

\[ F(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt. \]

Now consider

\[ \frac{F(x)}{x}. \]

Since

\[ F(x)\to0 \]

and

\[ x\to0 \]

as \(x\downarrow0\), by L’Hôpital’s rule,

\[ \lim_{x\downarrow0}\frac{F(x)}{x} = \lim_{x\downarrow0}f(x) = \lambda. \]

Therefore, for fixed \(x>0\),

\[ \frac{F(x/n)}{x/n}\to\lambda, \]

and hence

\[ nF\left(\frac{x}{n}\right) = x\frac{F(x/n)}{x/n} \to \lambda x. \]

Thus

\[ F\left(\frac{x}{n}\right) = \frac{nF(x/n)}{n}, \]

so

\[ \begin{aligned} \left( 1-F\left(\frac{x}{n}\right) \right)^{n-1} &= \left( 1-\frac{nF(x/n)}{n} \right)^{n-1}\\ &\longrightarrow e^{-\lambda x}. \end{aligned} \]

At the same time,

\[ f\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\to\lambda. \]

Therefore,

\[ g_n(x) \to \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, \qquad x>0. \]

Define

\[ g(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x} 1_{\{x>0\}}. \]

Then

\[ \int_{\mathbb R}g(x)\,dx = \int_0^\infty \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\,dx = 1, \]

so \(g\) is a probability density.

Since each \(g_n\) is also a probability density and

\[ g_n(x)\to g(x) \]

almost everywhere, Scheffé’s lemma gives

\[ \int_{\mathbb R} |g_n(x)-g(x)|\,dx \to0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ n\min_{1\leq i\leq n}X_i \Rightarrow \operatorname{Exp}(\lambda). } \]

\(\textbf{Note:}\)

The key local behavior is

\[ F(x)\sim\lambda x \qquad (x\downarrow0). \]

In my proof I obtained this by L’Hôpital’s rule:

\[ \frac{F(x)}{x} \to\lambda. \]

Under the usual interpretation that \(F'(x)=f(x)\) in a punctured neighborhood of \(0\), this is valid.

If one wants to avoid the differentiability issue for an arbitrary density, the same result follows directly from

\[ \frac{F(x)}{x} = \frac1x\int_0^x f(t)\,dt. \]

Since \(f(t)\to\lambda\), for every \(\varepsilon>0\), when \(x\) is sufficiently small,

\[ \lambda-\varepsilon \leq f(t) \leq \lambda+\varepsilon, \qquad 0<t<x, \]

and hence

\[ \lambda-\varepsilon \leq \frac{F(x)}{x} \leq \lambda+\varepsilon. \]

Thus again

\[ \frac{F(x)}{x}\to\lambda. \]

Ex 34

Suppose \(\{E_n,n\geq1\}\) are iid unit exponential random variables. Recall from Example 8.1.2 that

\[ \bigvee_{i=1}^n E_i-\log n\Rightarrow Y, \]

where \(Y\) has a Gumbel distribution.

Let \(\{W_n,n\geq1\}\) be iid Weibull random variables satisfying

\[ P[W_n>x]=e^{-x^\alpha}, \qquad \alpha>0,\ x>0. \]

Use the delta method to derive a weak limit theorem for

\[ \bigvee_{i=1}^n W_i. \]

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

For the Weibull random variable \(W_i\),

\[ P(W_i>x)=e^{-x^\alpha}, \qquad x>0. \]

Therefore, for \(x>0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} P(W_i^\alpha>x) &= P(W_i>x^{1/\alpha})\\ &= e^{-x}. \end{aligned} \]

Thus

\[ W_i^\alpha\overset{d}=E_i, \qquad E_i\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1), \]

or equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ W_i\overset{d}=E_i^{1/\alpha}. } \]

Define

\[ M_{E,n}:=\bigvee_{i=1}^nE_i \]

and

\[ M_{W,n}:=\bigvee_{i=1}^nW_i. \]

Since the function

\[ g(x)=x^{1/\alpha} \]

is increasing on \((0,\infty)\),

\[ \boxed{ M_{W,n} \overset{d}= g(M_{E,n}) = M_{E,n}^{1/\alpha}. } \]

From Example 8.1.2,

\[ M_{E,n}-\log n\Rightarrow Y, \]

where \(Y\) has the Gumbel distribution.

We therefore want to study

\[ g(M_{E,n})-g(\log n). \]

However, the ordinary delta method in Section 8.3.1 is stated around a fixed point, while here the centering point

\[ \theta_n=\log n \]

satisfies

\[ \theta_n\to\infty. \]

Thus we first justify the moving-center version of the delta method needed in this exercise.

A moving-center delta method

Suppose

\[ X_n-\theta_n\Rightarrow Y, \qquad \theta_n\to\infty. \]

Then

\[ X_n-\theta_n=O_P(1). \]

Since \(\theta_n\to\infty\),

\[ \frac{X_n-\theta_n}{\theta_n} \xrightarrow{P}0, \]

and therefore

\[ \boxed{ \frac{X_n}{\theta_n}\xrightarrow{P}1. } \]

Let \(g\) be differentiable, and suppose \(g'\) is eventually positive and regularly varying at infinity with index \(\rho\), that is,

\[ \frac{g'(tx)}{g'(x)} \longrightarrow t^\rho \qquad (x\to\infty) \]

for every \(t>0\).

By the mean value theorem, there exists a random \(\xi_n\) lying between \(X_n\) and \(\theta_n\) such that

\[ g(X_n)-g(\theta_n) = g'(\xi_n)(X_n-\theta_n). \]

Since \(\xi_n\) lies between \(X_n\) and \(\theta_n\),

\[ \left| \frac{\xi_n}{\theta_n}-1 \right| \leq \left| \frac{X_n}{\theta_n}-1 \right|. \]

Hence

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\xi_n}{\theta_n}\xrightarrow{P}1. } \]

Now write

\[ T_n:=\frac{\xi_n}{\theta_n}. \]

Then

\[ T_n\xrightarrow{P}1. \]

Since \(g'\) is regularly varying, the uniform convergence theorem for regularly varying functions implies that, uniformly for \(t\) in compact subsets of \((0,\infty)\),

\[ \frac{g'(t\theta_n)}{g'(\theta_n)} - t^\rho \to0. \]

Because \(T_n\to_P1\), with probability tending to one \(T_n\) lies in a compact neighborhood of \(1\). Therefore,

\[ \frac{g'(\xi_n)}{g'(\theta_n)} = \frac{g'(T_n\theta_n)}{g'(\theta_n)} \xrightarrow{P} 1^\rho = 1. \]

Hence,

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{g(X_n)-g(\theta_n)} {g'(\theta_n)} &= \frac{g'(\xi_n)} {g'(\theta_n)} (X_n-\theta_n). \end{aligned} \]

Since

\[ \frac{g'(\xi_n)}{g'(\theta_n)} \xrightarrow{P}1 \]

and

\[ X_n-\theta_n\Rightarrow Y, \]

Slutsky’s theorem gives

\[ \boxed{ \frac{g(X_n)-g(\theta_n)} {g'(\theta_n)} \Rightarrow Y. } \]

Thus regular variation of \(g'\) is a sufficient condition for this moving-center delta method.

Apply it to the Weibull maximum

In the present exercise,

\[ X_n=M_{E,n}, \qquad \theta_n=\log n, \]

and

\[ M_{E,n}-\log n\Rightarrow Y. \]

Take

\[ g(x)=x^{1/\alpha}. \]

Then

\[ g'(x) = \frac1\alpha x^{1/\alpha-1}. \]

Therefore \(g'\) is regularly varying with index

\[ \rho=\frac1\alpha-1. \]

Indeed, for every \(t>0\),

\[ \frac{g'(tx)}{g'(x)} = \frac{ \frac1\alpha(tx)^{1/\alpha-1} }{ \frac1\alpha x^{1/\alpha-1} } = t^{1/\alpha-1}. \]

Thus the moving-center delta method applies, and

\[ \frac{ g(M_{E,n})-g(\log n) }{ g'(\log n) } \Rightarrow Y. \]

Now

\[ g(M_{E,n}) = M_{E,n}^{1/\alpha} \overset{d}=M_{W,n}, \]

\[ g(\log n) = (\log n)^{1/\alpha}, \]

and

\[ g'(\log n) = \frac1\alpha (\log n)^{1/\alpha-1}. \]

Hence

\[ \boxed{ \frac{ M_{W,n}-(\log n)^{1/\alpha} }{ \frac1\alpha(\log n)^{1/\alpha-1} } \Rightarrow Y. } \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ \alpha(\log n)^{1-1/\alpha} \left[ M_{W,n}-(\log n)^{1/\alpha} \right] \Rightarrow Y, } \]

where \(Y\) has the Gumbel distribution.

\(\textbf{Note:}\)

Section 8.3.1 proves the delta method in a fixed-center setting. The proof there uses local linearization around a fixed parameter.

In this exercise the center is instead

\[ \theta_n=\log n\to\infty. \]

Therefore differentiability of \(g\) alone is not enough to justify

\[ g(M_{E,n})-g(\log n) \approx g'(\log n)(M_{E,n}-\log n). \]

The mean value theorem gives

\[ g(M_{E,n})-g(\log n) = g'(\xi_n)(M_{E,n}-\log n), \]

so what is really required is

\[ \frac{g'(\xi_n)}{g'(\log n)} \xrightarrow{P}1. \]

Since

\[ \frac{\xi_n}{\log n}\xrightarrow{P}1, \]

regular variation of \(g'\) provides exactly this stability.

For this exercise,

\[ g'(x) = \frac1\alpha x^{1/\alpha-1} \]

is regularly varying, and therefore the required derivative ratio converges to \(1\).

Thus the essential mechanism is

\[ M_{E,n}-\log n\Rightarrow Y \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \frac{M_{E,n}}{\log n}\to_P1 \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \frac{\xi_n}{\log n}\to_P1 \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \frac{g'(\xi_n)}{g'(\log n)}\to_P1 \qquad \text{(regular variation)} \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \frac{ g(M_{E,n})-g(\log n) }{ g'(\log n) } \Rightarrow Y. \]

In the step

\[ \frac{g(X_n)-g(\theta_n)}{g'(\theta_n)} = \frac{g'(\xi_n)}{g'(\theta_n)} (X_n-\theta_n), \]

where \(\xi_n\) lies between \(X_n\) and \(\theta_n\), suppose

\[ X_n-\theta_n\Rightarrow Y. \]

Then

\[ X_n-\theta_n=O_P(1). \]

Since \(\xi_n\) lies between \(X_n\) and \(\theta_n\),

\[ \xi_n-\theta_n=O_P(1). \]

Therefore, if we want

\[ \frac{g(X_n)-g(\theta_n)}{g'(\theta_n)} \Rightarrow Y, \]

it is enough to have

\[ \frac{g'(\xi_n)}{g'(\theta_n)} \xrightarrow{P}1. \]

Thus we only need a local property of \(g'\) in a bounded neighborhood of \(\theta_n\), rather than a global condition such as regular variation.

A convenient sufficient condition is that, for every \(M<\infty\),

\[ \boxed{ \sup_{|h|\leq M} \left| \frac{g'(\theta_n+h)}{g'(\theta_n)}-1 \right| \longrightarrow0. } \]

More generally, if \(\theta_n\to\infty\), one may write the condition as

\[ \boxed{ \forall M<\infty,\qquad \sup_{|h|\leq M} \left| \frac{g'(x+h)}{g'(x)}-1 \right| \longrightarrow0, \qquad x\to\infty. } \]

To see why this is sufficient, write

\[ \xi_n=\theta_n+H_n, \]

where

\[ H_n=O_P(1). \]

For any \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(M<\infty\) such that, for all sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ P(|H_n|>M)<\varepsilon. \]

On the event \(\{|H_n|\leq M\}\),

\[ \left| \frac{g'(\xi_n)}{g'(\theta_n)}-1 \right| = \left| \frac{g'(\theta_n+H_n)}{g'(\theta_n)}-1 \right| \leq \sup_{|h|\leq M} \left| \frac{g'(\theta_n+h)}{g'(\theta_n)}-1 \right|. \]

By the local stability assumption, the right-hand side converges to \(0\). Therefore,

\[ \frac{g'(\xi_n)}{g'(\theta_n)} \xrightarrow{P}1. \]

Since

\[ X_n-\theta_n\Rightarrow Y, \]

Slutsky’s theorem gives

\[ \boxed{ \frac{g(X_n)-g(\theta_n)} {g'(\theta_n)} \Rightarrow Y. } \]

So the moving-center delta method used in Exercise 34 does not require a global condition on \(g'\). What the proof actually needs is only that \(g'\) be asymptotically stable on bounded additive neighborhoods of \(\theta_n\).

Ex 16

Suppose \(\{X_n,n\geq1\}\) are iid non-negative random variables and set

\[ M_n=\bigvee_{i=1}^n X_i,\qquad n\geq1. \]

Show that there exists a sequence \(a_n>0\) such that, for \(x>0\),

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty} P\left[\frac{M_n}{a_n}\leq x\right] = \exp\{-x^{-\alpha}\}, \qquad x>0,\ \alpha>0, \]

iff the regular variation condition holds:

\[ \lim_{t\to\infty} \frac{P[X_1>tx]}{P[X_1>t]} = x^{-\alpha}, \qquad x>0. \tag{8.30} \]

In this case, what limit distribution exists for \(\log M_n\)? For \(M_n^2\)?

Verify (8.30) for the Cauchy density and the Pareto distribution?

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

Let

\[ S(x):=P(X_1>x)=1-F(x) \]

be the survival function.

We first prove the equivalence

\[ \boxed{ \frac{M_n}{a_n}\Rightarrow\Phi_\alpha \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S\in RV_{-\alpha}, } \]

where

\[ \Phi_\alpha(x)=\exp\{-x^{-\alpha}\}, \qquad x>0. \]

\((\Rightarrow)\)

Suppose there exists \(a_n>0\) such that

\[ \frac{M_n}{a_n}\Rightarrow\Phi_\alpha. \]

Then, for fixed \(x>0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} P(M_n\leq a_nx) &= F(a_nx)^n\\ &= [1-S(a_nx)]^n\\ &\longrightarrow \exp\{-x^{-\alpha}\}. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ nS(a_nx)\longrightarrow x^{-\alpha}. } \tag{1} \]

In particular, taking \(x=1\),

\[ \boxed{ nS(a_n)\longrightarrow1. } \tag{2} \]

Hence

\[ S(a_n)\to0, \]

and from (1) and (2),

\[ \boxed{ \frac{S(a_nx)}{S(a_n)} \longrightarrow x^{-\alpha}. } \tag{3} \]

For now, however, (3) only tells us the behavior of \(S\) along the sequence \(\{a_n\}\). Our real goal is

\[ \frac{S(tx)}{S(t)} \longrightarrow x^{-\alpha}, \qquad t\to\infty. \]

So we need a bridge from \(\{a_n\}\) to arbitrary large \(t\).

Step 1: \(a_n\to\infty\)

Let

\[ x_F:=\sup\{x:S(x)>0\} \]

be the right endpoint of the distribution.

Suppose \(a_n\not\to\infty\). Since \(a_n>0\), there exists a subsequence \(\{a_{n_k}\}\) such that

\[ a_{n_k}\to a<\infty. \]

Since

\[ S(a_{n_k})\to0, \]

we consider the position of \(a\) relative to \(x_F\).

If \(a<x_F\), choose \(y\) such that

\[ a<y<x_F. \]

Then

\[ S(y)>0. \]

For sufficiently large \(k\),

\[ a_{n_k}<y, \]

and since \(S\) is non-increasing,

\[ S(a_{n_k})\geq S(y)>0, \]

contradicting \(S(a_{n_k})\to0\).

If \(a>x_F\), then for sufficiently large \(k\),

\[ a_{n_k}>x_F, \]

and therefore

\[ S(a_{n_k})=0, \]

contradicting

\[ n_kS(a_{n_k})\to1. \]

The remaining possibility is

\[ a=x_F. \]

Take any \(x\in(0,1)\). Then

\[ a_{n_k}x\to ax<x_F. \]

Choose \(y\) such that

\[ ax<y<x_F. \]

For sufficiently large \(k\),

\[ a_{n_k}x<y, \]

so

\[ S(a_{n_k}x)\geq S(y)>0. \]

But from (1),

\[ n_kS(a_{n_k}x)\to x^{-\alpha}, \]

which implies

\[ S(a_{n_k}x)\to0. \]

This is again a contradiction.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ a_n\to\infty. } \tag{4} \]

Step 2: \(a_{n+1}/a_n\to1\)

By assumption,

\[ \frac{M_{n+1}}{a_{n+1}} \Rightarrow Z, \qquad Z\sim\Phi_\alpha. \]

We also have

\[ \frac{M_{n+1}}{a_n} \Rightarrow Z. \]

Indeed,

\[ \begin{aligned} P(M_{n+1}\leq a_nx) &= F(a_nx)^{n+1}\\ &= F(a_nx)^nF(a_nx). \end{aligned} \]

We know

\[ F(a_nx)^n\to\Phi_\alpha(x), \]

and by (1),

\[ S(a_nx)\to0, \]

so

\[ F(a_nx)\to1. \]

Hence

\[ \frac{M_{n+1}}{a_n} \Rightarrow Z. \]

Thus

\[ \frac{M_{n+1}}{a_{n+1}} \Rightarrow Z \]

and

\[ \frac{M_{n+1}}{a_n} \Rightarrow Z. \]

By the convergence to types theorem, there exists \(A>0\) such that

\[ \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\to A. \]

Then

\[ AZ\overset{d}=Z. \]

Since

\[ P(Z\leq x) = \exp\{-x^{-\alpha}\}, \]

we get

\[ \begin{aligned} P(AZ\leq x) &= P\left(Z\leq\frac{x}{A}\right)\\ &= \exp\left\{ -\left(\frac{x}{A}\right)^{-\alpha} \right\}\\ &= \exp\{-A^\alpha x^{-\alpha}\}. \end{aligned} \]

But \(AZ\overset d=Z\), so

\[ A^\alpha=1. \]

Since \(A>0\),

\[ A=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\to1. } \tag{5} \]

So far, \(\{a_n\}\) has the two properties we want:

\[ a_n\to\infty \]

and

\[ \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\to1. \]

However, \(\{a_n\}\) need not be monotone. To squeeze an arbitrary \(t\) between neighboring normalization points, we now construct a monotone sequence which behaves asymptotically like \(\{a_n\}\).

Step 3: Monotonization of \(\{a_n\}\)

Define

\[ b_n := F^{\leftarrow}\left(1-\frac1n\right) = \inf\left\{ x:S(x)\leq\frac1n \right\}. \]

Since \(1/n\) decreases with \(n\) and \(S\) is non-increasing,

\[ \boxed{ \{b_n\}\text{ is non-decreasing}. } \]

We now show that

\[ \boxed{ \frac{b_n}{a_n}\to1. } \]

Fix \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\).

From (1),

\[ nS((1+\varepsilon)a_n) \longrightarrow (1+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha}<1. \]

Hence, for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ S((1+\varepsilon)a_n)<\frac1n. \]

Since \(b_n\) is the first point where \(S\) reaches or falls below \(1/n\),

\[ b_n\leq(1+\varepsilon)a_n. \]

Similarly,

\[ nS((1-\varepsilon)a_n) \longrightarrow (1-\varepsilon)^{-\alpha}>1. \]

Hence eventually

\[ S((1-\varepsilon)a_n)>\frac1n. \]

Therefore the \(1/n\) level has not yet been crossed at \((1-\varepsilon)a_n\), so

\[ (1-\varepsilon)a_n<b_n. \]

Thus

\[ 1-\varepsilon < \frac{b_n}{a_n} \leq 1+\varepsilon. \]

Since \(\varepsilon>0\) is arbitrary,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{b_n}{a_n}\to1. } \tag{6} \]

So \(\{b_n\}\) is a monotonization of \(\{a_n\}\).

From (4) and (6),

\[ b_n\to\infty. \]

Also,

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{b_{n+1}}{b_n} &= \frac{b_{n+1}}{a_{n+1}} \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \frac{a_n}{b_n}\\ &\longrightarrow1. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{b_{n+1}}{b_n}\to1. } \tag{7} \]

Step 4: Transfer the tail behavior from \(a_n\) to \(b_n\)

From

\[ (1-\varepsilon)a_n<b_n\leq(1+\varepsilon)a_n \]

and the fact that \(S\) is non-increasing,

\[ S((1+\varepsilon)a_n) \leq S(b_n) \leq S((1-\varepsilon)a_n). \]

Divide by \(S(a_n)\):

\[ \frac{S((1+\varepsilon)a_n)}{S(a_n)} \leq \frac{S(b_n)}{S(a_n)} \leq \frac{S((1-\varepsilon)a_n)}{S(a_n)}. \]

Using (3),

\[ (1+\varepsilon)^{-\alpha} \leq \liminf_{n\to\infty} \frac{S(b_n)}{S(a_n)} \]

and

\[ \limsup_{n\to\infty} \frac{S(b_n)}{S(a_n)} \leq (1-\varepsilon)^{-\alpha}. \]

Letting \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\) gives

\[ \boxed{ \frac{S(b_n)}{S(a_n)}\to1. } \]

Together with (2),

\[ \boxed{ nS(b_n)\to1. } \tag{8} \]

Now take fixed \(x>0\).

Again,

\[ (1-\varepsilon)a_n<b_n\leq(1+\varepsilon)a_n \]

implies

\[ S((1+\varepsilon)a_nx) \leq S(b_nx) \leq S((1-\varepsilon)a_nx). \]

Multiplying by \(n\) and using (1),

\[ ((1+\varepsilon)x)^{-\alpha} \leq \liminf_{n\to\infty}nS(b_nx) \]

and

\[ \limsup_{n\to\infty}nS(b_nx) \leq ((1-\varepsilon)x)^{-\alpha}. \]

Letting \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\),

\[ \boxed{ nS(b_nx)\to x^{-\alpha}. } \tag{9} \]

Combining (8) and (9),

\[ \boxed{ \frac{S(b_nx)}{S(b_n)} \to x^{-\alpha}. } \tag{10} \]

Thus \(\{b_n\}\) carries the same asymptotic tail behavior as \(\{a_n\}\), but it also has the monotonicity that we need.

Step 5: Extend the result from \(\{b_n\}\) to arbitrary \(t\to\infty\)

For sufficiently large \(t\), define

\[ n(t) := \max\{n:b_n\leq t\}. \]

Since \(b_n\to\infty\), this is well-defined for sufficiently large \(t\), and

\[ b_{n(t)} \leq t < b_{n(t)+1}. \]

For simplicity, write \(n=n(t)\).

Since \(S\) is non-increasing,

\[ S(b_{n+1}x) \leq S(tx) \leq S(b_nx), \]

and

\[ S(b_{n+1}) \leq S(t) \leq S(b_n). \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{S(b_{n+1}x)}{S(b_n)} \leq \frac{S(tx)}{S(t)} \leq \frac{S(b_nx)}{S(b_{n+1})}. \]

For the lower bound,

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{S(b_{n+1}x)}{S(b_n)} &= \frac{S(b_{n+1}x)}{S(b_{n+1})} \frac{S(b_{n+1})}{S(b_n)}. \end{aligned} \]

By (10),

\[ \frac{S(b_{n+1}x)}{S(b_{n+1})} \to x^{-\alpha}. \]

Also, from (8),

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{S(b_{n+1})}{S(b_n)} &= \frac{(n+1)S(b_{n+1})}{nS(b_n)} \frac{n}{n+1}\\ &\to1. \end{aligned} \]

Hence the lower bound tends to \(x^{-\alpha}\).

Similarly,

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{S(b_nx)}{S(b_{n+1})} &= \frac{S(b_nx)}{S(b_n)} \frac{S(b_n)}{S(b_{n+1})}\\ &\to x^{-\alpha}. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, by squeezing,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{S(tx)}{S(t)} \to x^{-\alpha}, \qquad t\to\infty. } \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{ S\in RV_{-\alpha}. } \]

\((\Leftarrow)\)

Now suppose

\[ \boxed{ \frac{S(tx)}{S(t)} \to x^{-\alpha}, \qquad t\to\infty, } \tag{11} \]

for every fixed \(x>0\).

We want to construct \(a_n>0\) such that

\[ \frac{M_n}{a_n}\Rightarrow\Phi_\alpha. \]

Define

\[ a_n := F^{\leftarrow}\left(1-\frac1n\right) = \inf\left\{ x:S(x)\leq\frac1n \right\}. \]

The regular variation condition implies that \(S\) has no finite right endpoint. Hence, for every finite \(M\),

\[ S(M)>0. \]

For sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ \frac1n<S(M), \]

so the \(1/n\) crossing occurs to the right of \(M\):

\[ a_n>M. \]

Since \(M\) is arbitrary,

\[ \boxed{ a_n\to\infty. } \tag{12} \]

By the definition of \(a_n\), for every \(t<a_n\),

\[ S(t)>\frac1n. \]

Also, since \(S\) is right-continuous,

\[ \boxed{ S(a_n)\leq\frac1n. } \]

Indeed, by the definition of the infimum, there exists a sequence \(x_m\downarrow a_n\) such that

\[ S(x_m)\leq\frac1n. \]

Right continuity of \(S\) then gives

\[ S(a_n) = \lim_{m\to\infty}S(x_m) \leq \frac1n. \]

Thus

\[ \limsup_{n\to\infty}nS(a_n)\leq1. \]

To obtain the lower bound, fix \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\).

Since

\[ (1-\varepsilon)a_n<a_n, \]

the definition of \(a_n\) gives

\[ S((1-\varepsilon)a_n)>\frac1n. \]

Therefore,

\[ nS((1-\varepsilon)a_n)>1. \]

Now, because \(a_n\to\infty\), regular variation gives

\[ \frac{S((1-\varepsilon)a_n)}{S(a_n)} \to (1-\varepsilon)^{-\alpha}. \]

Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} nS(a_n) &= nS((1-\varepsilon)a_n) \frac{S(a_n)}{S((1-\varepsilon)a_n)}. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \liminf_{n\to\infty}nS(a_n) \geq (1-\varepsilon)^\alpha. \]

Letting \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\),

\[ \liminf_{n\to\infty}nS(a_n)\geq1. \]

Together with the upper bound,

\[ \boxed{ nS(a_n)\to1. } \tag{13} \]

Now, for fixed \(x>0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} nS(a_nx) &= nS(a_n) \frac{S(a_nx)}{S(a_n)}\\ &\longrightarrow 1\cdot x^{-\alpha}, \end{aligned} \]

by (11) and (13).

Thus

\[ \boxed{ nS(a_nx)\to x^{-\alpha}. } \]

Finally,

\[ \begin{aligned} P\left(\frac{M_n}{a_n}\leq x\right) &= P(M_n\leq a_nx)\\ &= F(a_nx)^n\\ &= [1-S(a_nx)]^n\\ &\longrightarrow \exp\{-x^{-\alpha}\}. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{M_n}{a_n} \Rightarrow \Phi_\alpha. } \]

Combining the two directions,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{M_n}{a_n}\Rightarrow\Phi_\alpha \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S\in RV_{-\alpha}. } \]

\(\textbf{Note:}\)

The difficult part of the \((\Rightarrow)\) direction is the difference between

\[ \frac{S(a_nx)}{S(a_n)} \to x^{-\alpha} \]

and the actual definition of regular variation,

\[ \frac{S(tx)}{S(t)} \to x^{-\alpha} \qquad(t\to\infty). \]

The first statement only gives the asymptotic behavior along the normalization sequence \(\{a_n\}\).

The bridge is

\[ \frac{M_n}{a_n}\Rightarrow\Phi_\alpha \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ nS(a_nx)\to x^{-\alpha} \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ a_n\to\infty, \qquad \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\to1 \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \text{construct the monotone sequence } b_n=F^{\leftarrow}\left(1-\frac1n\right) \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \frac{b_n}{a_n}\to1 \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \frac{S(b_nx)}{S(b_n)} \to x^{-\alpha} \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \text{squeeze arbitrary }t \text{ between }b_n\text{ and }b_{n+1} \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ S\in RV_{-\alpha}. \]

Thus \(\{b_n\}\) acts as a monotonization of the original normalization sequence \(\{a_n\}\).

In the \((\Leftarrow)\) direction, one should not simply assume

\[ S(a_n)=\frac1n, \]

because the distribution may have jumps. The correct argument uses the first crossing definition of \(a_n\), right continuity of \(S\), and an \(\varepsilon\)-squeeze to prove

\[ nS(a_n)\to1. \]

Ex 28

Let \(x\in(0,1)\) have binary expansion

\[ x=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{d_n}{2^n}. \]

Set

\[ f_n(x)= \begin{cases} 2, & d_n=0,\\ 1, & d_n=1. \end{cases} \]

Then show

\[ \int_0^1 f_n(x)\,dx=1 \]

so that \(f_n\) is a density. The sequence \(f_n\) only converges on a set of Lebesgue measure \(0\). If \(X_n\) is a random variable with density \(f_n\), then

\[ X_n\Rightarrow U, \]

where \(U\) is \(U(0,1)\).

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

\(\textbf{Typo in the exercise:}\)

The second branch of \(f_n\) is printed as \(1\), but this cannot be correct, since then

\[ \int_0^1 f_n(x)\,dx = 2\cdot\frac12+1\cdot\frac12 = \frac32. \]

The intended definition should be

\[ \boxed{ f_n(x)= \begin{cases} 2, & d_n=0,\\ 0, & d_n=1. \end{cases} } \]

We use this corrected version below.

For every fixed \(n\), the \(n\)-th binary digit is \(0\) on half of \((0,1)\) and \(1\) on the other half. Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^1f_n(x)\,dx &= 2\,\lambda\{x:d_n(x)=0\} + 0\,\lambda\{x:d_n(x)=1\}\\ &= 2\cdot\frac12\\ &= 1. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(f_n\) is a density.

Now \(f_n(x)\in\{0,2\}\). Therefore, for fixed \(x\), the sequence \(\{f_n(x)\}\) converges only if it is eventually constant, which means that the binary expansion of \(x\) is eventually all \(0\)’s or eventually all \(1\)’s.

The collection of such \(x\) is countable. Hence

\[ \boxed{ \lambda\{x:f_n(x)\text{ converges}\}=0. } \]

It remains to show that nevertheless

\[ X_n\Rightarrow U(0,1). \]

Define

\[ f_n(x)=1+h_n(x), \]

where

\[ h_n(x)= \begin{cases} 1, & d_n(x)=0,\\ -1, & d_n(x)=1. \end{cases} \]

Let \(g\) be any continuous function on \([0,1]\). Since \([0,1]\) is compact, \(g\) is uniformly continuous.

We have

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^1g(x)f_n(x)\,dx &= \int_0^1g(x)\,dx + \int_0^1g(x)h_n(x)\,dx. \end{aligned} \]

Since \(1_{[0,1]}\) is the density of \(U(0,1)\), it is enough to prove

\[ \boxed{ \int_0^1g(x)h_n(x)\,dx\to0. } \]

For each \(n\), divide \([0,1)\) into \(2^n\) subintervals

\[ I_{j,n} = \left[ \frac{j}{2^n}, \frac{j+1}{2^n} \right), \qquad j=0,\ldots,2^n-1. \]

Each interval has length \(2^{-n}\).

For \(n=1\), the binary digit \(d_1\) is \(0\) on the first interval and \(1\) on the second interval, so \(h_1\) takes the values

\[ 1,-1. \]

For \(n=2\), the four intervals correspond to the binary beginnings

\[ 00,\ 01,\ 10,\ 11, \]

so again the second digit alternates between \(0\) and \(1\), and \(h_2\) takes the values

\[ 1,-1,1,-1. \]

In general, \(h_n\) alternates between \(1\) and \(-1\) on consecutive intervals:

\[ 1,-1,1,-1,\ldots. \]

Therefore we pair the intervals

\[ I_{2j,n},\qquad I_{2j+1,n}, \qquad j=0,\ldots,2^{n-1}-1. \]

On the first interval of each pair,

\[ h_n=1, \]

while on the second,

\[ h_n=-1. \]

Hence the contribution from one pair is

\[ \begin{aligned} &\int_{I_{2j,n}}g(x)h_n(x)\,dx + \int_{I_{2j+1,n}}g(x)h_n(x)\,dx\\ &= \int_{I_{2j,n}}g(x)\,dx - \int_{I_{2j+1,n}}g(x)\,dx. \end{aligned} \]

By the integral mean-value theorem, there exist

\[ \xi_{j,0}\in I_{2j,n}, \qquad \xi_{j,1}\in I_{2j+1,n}, \]

such that

\[ \int_{I_{2j,n}}g(x)\,dx = \frac1{2^n}g(\xi_{j,0}), \]

and

\[ \int_{I_{2j+1,n}}g(x)\,dx = \frac1{2^n}g(\xi_{j,1}). \]

Therefore,

\[ \int_{I_{2j,n}}g(x)h_n(x)\,dx + \int_{I_{2j+1,n}}g(x)h_n(x)\,dx = \frac1{2^n} \left[ g(\xi_{j,0})-g(\xi_{j,1}) \right]. \]

There are \(2^{n-1}\) such pairs.

Also, since the two points lie in adjacent intervals,

\[ |\xi_{j,0}-\xi_{j,1}| \leq \frac{2}{2^n} = 2^{1-n}. \]

Since \(g\) is uniformly continuous, for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there exists \(\delta>0\) such that

\[ |x-y|<\delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |g(x)-g(y)|<\varepsilon. \]

For sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ 2^{1-n}<\delta. \]

Thus, uniformly over all \(j\),

\[ |g(\xi_{j,0})-g(\xi_{j,1})| < \varepsilon. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \left| \int_0^1g(x)h_n(x)\,dx \right| &\leq \sum_{j=0}^{2^{n-1}-1} \frac1{2^n} \left| g(\xi_{j,0})-g(\xi_{j,1}) \right|\\ &\leq 2^{n-1}\frac1{2^n}\varepsilon\\ &= \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\varepsilon>0\) is arbitrary,

\[ \boxed{ \int_0^1g(x)h_n(x)\,dx\to0. } \]

Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^1g(x)f_n(x)\,dx &= \int_0^1g(x)\,dx + \int_0^1g(x)h_n(x)\,dx\\ &\longrightarrow \int_0^1g(x)\,dx. \end{aligned} \]

That is,

\[ E[g(X_n)] \longrightarrow E[g(U)] \]

for every continuous \(g\) on \([0,1]\).

Therefore, by the Portmanteau theorem,

\[ \boxed{ X_n\Rightarrow U(0,1). } \]

\(\textbf{Note:}\)

This exercise gives an example where the densities themselves behave very badly pointwise:

\[ f_n(x)\not\to1 \]

for Lebesgue-a.e. \(x\).

In fact,

\[ |f_n(x)-1|=1 \]

for every \(x\) outside the irrelevant binary-expansion ambiguity points, so

\[ \int_0^1|f_n(x)-1|\,dx=1 \]

for every \(n\).

Thus there is neither almost-everywhere convergence of the densities nor \(L^1\) convergence to the uniform density.

Nevertheless,

\[ \int_0^1g(x)f_n(x)\,dx \to \int_0^1g(x)\,dx \]

for every continuous test function \(g\).

The reason is that the \(+1\) and \(-1\) oscillations of \(h_n\) occur on smaller and smaller dyadic intervals. Uniform continuity makes \(g\) almost constant across each neighboring pair, so the two contributions cancel asymptotically.

Ex 11

Lévy metric. For two probability distributions \(F,G\), define

\[ d(F,G) := \inf\left\{ \delta>0: \forall x\in\mathbb R,\ F(x-\delta)-\delta \leq G(x) \leq F(x+\delta)+\delta \right\}. \]

Show this is a metric on the space of probability distribution functions which metrizes weak convergence; that is,

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F_0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad d(F_n,F_0)\to0. \]

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

For convenience, define the set of admissible values

\[ D(F,G) := \left\{ \delta>0: \forall x\in\mathbb R,\ F(x-\delta)-\delta \leq G(x) \leq F(x+\delta)+\delta \right\}. \]

Then

\[ d(F,G)=\inf D(F,G). \]

Notice first that \(D(F,G)\) is non-empty. For example, every \(\delta>1\) is admissible, since all distribution functions take values in \([0,1]\). Therefore \(d(F,G)\) is well-defined and non-negative.

Part 1: \(d\) is a metric

(i) Symmetry

Let

\[ \delta\in D(F,G). \]

Then, for every \(x\in\mathbb R\),

\[ F(x-\delta)-\delta \leq G(x) \leq F(x+\delta)+\delta. \tag{1} \]

We want to show that the same \(\delta\) is also admissible for \(d(G,F)\).

From the upper inequality in (1), replace \(x\) by \(x-\delta\):

\[ G(x-\delta) \leq F(x)+\delta. \]

Hence

\[ G(x-\delta)-\delta \leq F(x). \]

From the lower inequality in (1), replace \(x\) by \(x+\delta\):

\[ F(x)-\delta \leq G(x+\delta). \]

Therefore,

\[ F(x) \leq G(x+\delta)+\delta. \]

Thus

\[ G(x-\delta)-\delta \leq F(x) \leq G(x+\delta)+\delta, \]

so

\[ \delta\in D(G,F). \]

Therefore,

\[ D(F,G)\subseteq D(G,F). \]

By reversing the roles of \(F\) and \(G\),

\[ D(G,F)\subseteq D(F,G). \]

Hence

\[ D(F,G)=D(G,F), \]

and therefore

\[ \boxed{ d(F,G)=d(G,F). } \]

(ii) Identity of indiscernibles

First suppose

\[ F=G. \]

For every \(\delta>0\), since \(F\) is non-decreasing,

\[ F(x-\delta)-\delta \leq F(x) \leq F(x+\delta)+\delta. \]

Thus every \(\delta>0\) is admissible, so

\[ \boxed{ d(F,F)=0. } \]

Conversely, suppose

\[ d(F,G)=0. \]

Since the infimum of \(D(F,G)\) is \(0\), we can choose a sequence \(\delta_k\in D(F,G)\) such that

\[ \delta_k\downarrow0. \]

For every \(x\),

\[ G(x) \leq F(x+\delta_k)+\delta_k. \]

Since \(F\) is right-continuous,

\[ F(x+\delta_k)\to F(x), \]

so

\[ G(x)\leq F(x). \]

By symmetry,

\[ d(G,F)=0. \]

Thus we may similarly choose admissible values tending to \(0\) for \((G,F)\), which gives

\[ F(x)\leq G(x). \]

Therefore,

\[ F(x)=G(x) \qquad \forall x\in\mathbb R. \]

Hence

\[ \boxed{ d(F,G)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F=G. } \]

(iii) Triangle inequality

Let \(F,G,H\) be probability distribution functions.

Fix \(\varepsilon>0\). Choose admissible \(\delta_1,\delta_2\) such that

\[ \delta_1 < d(F,G)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}, \]

and

\[ \delta_2 < d(G,H)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Since \(\delta_1\in D(F,G)\),

\[ F(x-\delta_1)-\delta_1 \leq G(x) \leq F(x+\delta_1)+\delta_1. \tag{2} \]

Since \(\delta_2\in D(G,H)\),

\[ G(x-\delta_2)-\delta_2 \leq H(x) \leq G(x+\delta_2)+\delta_2. \tag{3} \]

For the upper bound, substitute \(x+\delta_2\) into the upper inequality of (2):

\[ G(x+\delta_2) \leq F(x+\delta_2+\delta_1)+\delta_1. \]

Using (3),

\[ \begin{aligned} H(x) &\leq G(x+\delta_2)+\delta_2\\ &\leq F(x+\delta_1+\delta_2) +\delta_1+\delta_2. \end{aligned} \]

For the lower bound, substitute \(x-\delta_2\) into the lower inequality of (2):

\[ F(x-\delta_2-\delta_1)-\delta_1 \leq G(x-\delta_2). \]

Using (3),

\[ \begin{aligned} H(x) &\geq G(x-\delta_2)-\delta_2\\ &\geq F(x-\delta_1-\delta_2) -(\delta_1+\delta_2). \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ F(x-(\delta_1+\delta_2)) -(\delta_1+\delta_2) \leq H(x) \leq F(x+(\delta_1+\delta_2)) +(\delta_1+\delta_2). \]

Thus

\[ \delta_1+\delta_2\in D(F,H), \]

and hence

\[ d(F,H) \leq \delta_1+\delta_2. \]

Therefore,

\[ d(F,H) < d(F,G)+d(G,H)+\varepsilon. \]

Since \(\varepsilon>0\) is arbitrary,

\[ \boxed{ d(F,H) \leq d(F,G)+d(G,H). } \]

Thus \(d\) is a metric.

Part 2: \(d\) metrizes weak convergence

We prove

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad d(F_n,F)\to0. \]

\((\Rightarrow)\) Suppose \(F_n\Rightarrow F\)

We want to prove

\[ d(F_n,F)\to0. \]

Fix

\[ 0<\varepsilon<1. \]

We need to show that, for all sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ F(x-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon \leq F_n(x) \leq F(x+\varepsilon)+\varepsilon \tag{4} \]

for all \(x\in\mathbb R\) simultaneously.

The difficulty is that weak convergence only gives

\[ F_n(x)\to F(x) \]

at individual continuity points of \(F\). Therefore we need to turn the pointwise information into a finite collection of conditions.

First choose continuity points

\[ A<B \]

of \(F\) such that

\[ F(A)<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}, \qquad F(B)>1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}. \]

This is possible because

\[ F(x)\to0 \qquad (x\to-\infty), \]

and

\[ F(x)\to1 \qquad (x\to+\infty), \]

while the set of discontinuities of a distribution function is at most countable.

Inside the compact interval \([A,B]\), choose finitely many continuity points

\[ A=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_m=B \]

such that

\[ x_{j+1}-x_j<\frac{\varepsilon}{2} \qquad (j=0,\ldots,m-1). \]

Since

\[ F_n(x_j)\to F(x_j) \]

for every \(j\), and there are only finitely many \(x_j\)’s, there exists one common \(N\) such that, whenever \(n\geq N\),

\[ |F_n(x_j)-F(x_j)| < \frac{\varepsilon}{4} \qquad (j=0,\ldots,m). \tag{5} \]

We now verify (4) in three regions.

Middle region: \(A\leq x\leq B\)

Choose \(j\) such that

\[ x_j\leq x\leq x_{j+1}. \]

Since \(F_n\) is non-decreasing,

\[ F_n(x_j) \leq F_n(x) \leq F_n(x_{j+1}). \]

For the lower bound, by (5),

\[ F_n(x) \geq F_n(x_j) > F(x_j)-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}. \]

Since

\[ x-x_j<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<\varepsilon, \]

we have

\[ x-\varepsilon<x_j. \]

Hence, by monotonicity of \(F\),

\[ F(x-\varepsilon) \leq F(x_j). \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} F_n(x) &> F(x_j)-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\\ &\geq F(x-\varepsilon)-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\\ &\geq F(x-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon. \end{aligned} \]

For the upper bound, again by (5),

\[ F_n(x) \leq F_n(x_{j+1}) < F(x_{j+1})+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}. \]

Since

\[ x_{j+1}-x<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<\varepsilon, \]

we have

\[ x_{j+1}<x+\varepsilon. \]

Thus

\[ F(x_{j+1}) \leq F(x+\varepsilon). \]

Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} F_n(x) &< F(x_{j+1})+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\\ &\leq F(x+\varepsilon)+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\\ &\leq F(x+\varepsilon)+\varepsilon. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore (4) holds for every \(x\in[A,B]\).

Left tail: \(x<A\)

For the lower bound,

\[ F(x-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon \leq F(A)-\varepsilon < 0 \leq F_n(x). \]

For the upper bound,

\[ F_n(x) \leq F_n(A). \]

By (5),

\[ F_n(A) < F(A)+\frac{\varepsilon}{4} < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Since

\[ F(x+\varepsilon)\geq0, \]

we get

\[ F_n(x) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \leq F(x+\varepsilon)+\varepsilon. \]

Thus (4) also holds for \(x<A\).

Right tail: \(x>B\)

For the upper bound,

\[ F_n(x)\leq1. \]

Since

\[ F(x+\varepsilon) \geq F(B) > 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}, \]

we certainly have

\[ F_n(x) \leq F(x+\varepsilon)+\varepsilon. \]

For the lower bound,

\[ F_n(x) \geq F_n(B). \]

By (5),

\[ F_n(B) > F(B)-\frac{\varepsilon}{4} > 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

On the other hand,

\[ F(x-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon \leq 1-\varepsilon < 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ F(x-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon < F_n(x). \]

Thus (4) holds also for \(x>B\).

We have therefore shown that, for all \(n\geq N\),

\[ F(x-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon \leq F_n(x) \leq F(x+\varepsilon)+\varepsilon \qquad \forall x\in\mathbb R. \]

Hence \(\varepsilon\) is admissible for \(d(F_n,F)\), so

\[ d(F_n,F)\leq\varepsilon. \]

Since \(\varepsilon>0\) is arbitrary,

\[ \boxed{ d(F_n,F)\to0. } \]

\((\Leftarrow)\) Suppose \(d(F_n,F)\to0\)

For each \(n\), choose an admissible \(\delta_n\) such that

\[ \delta_n < d(F_n,F)+\frac1n. \]

Then

\[ \delta_n\to0. \]

Since \(\delta_n\) is admissible,

\[ F(x-\delta_n)-\delta_n \leq F_n(x) \leq F(x+\delta_n)+\delta_n \qquad \forall x\in\mathbb R. \tag{6} \]

Now fix

\[ x\in C(F), \]

where \(C(F)\) denotes the set of continuity points of \(F\).

Since

\[ \delta_n\to0 \]

and \(F\) is continuous at \(x\),

\[ F(x-\delta_n)\to F(x) \]

and

\[ F(x+\delta_n)\to F(x). \]

Therefore the lower and upper bounds in (6) both converge to \(F(x)\).

By squeezing,

\[ F_n(x)\to F(x). \]

Thus

\[ F_n(x)\to F(x) \qquad \forall x\in C(F), \]

which is exactly

\[ \boxed{ F_n\Rightarrow F. } \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ F_n\Rightarrow F \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad d(F_n,F)\to0. } \]

Hence the Lévy metric metrizes weak convergence.

\(\textbf{Note:}\)

The main difficulty in the direction

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d(F_n,F)\to0 \]

is the difference between a pointwise statement and the requirement in the definition of the Lévy metric.

Weak convergence gives

\[ F_n(x)\to F(x) \]

only for each fixed \(x\in C(F)\), whereas the Lévy metric requires one \(\varepsilon\)-inequality that holds for

\[ \forall x\in\mathbb R \]

simultaneously.

Choosing \(x_L,x_R\) separately for each \(x\) therefore gives an \(N=N(x,\varepsilon)\), which is not enough.

The repair is to choose finitely many continuity points on a compact middle interval. Since there are only finitely many of them, one common \(N\) works at all grid points. Monotonicity then fills the gaps between grid points, while the two tails are controlled by choosing \(A\) and \(B\) sufficiently far out.

Thus the key bridge is

\[ \text{pointwise convergence at continuity points} \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \text{finite continuity grid} \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \text{one common }N \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \text{uniform Lévy inequalities for all }x. \]

Ex 35

Suppose \(\{F_n,n\geq 0\}\) are probability distributions such that

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F_0. \]

For \(t>0\), let

\[ u_t(\cdot):\mathbb R\to\mathbb R \]

be an equicontinuous family of functions that are uniformly bounded; that is,

\[ \sup_{t>0,\ x\in\mathbb R}|u_t(x)|\leq M \]

for some constant \(M\). Then show

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_{\mathbb R}u_t(x)F_n(dx) = \int_{\mathbb R}u_t(x)F_0(dx) \]

uniformly in \(t\).

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

What we want to show is

\[ \boxed{ \sup_{t>0} \left| \int_{\mathbb R}u_t(x)F_n(dx) - \int_{\mathbb R}u_t(x)F_0(dx) \right| \to0. } \]

The idea is to first control the tails by uniform boundedness, and then use equicontinuity to reduce the compact middle part to finitely many interval probabilities.

Fix \(\eta>0\).

Step 1: Control the tails

Since

\[ F_n\Rightarrow F_0, \]

choose \(K>0\) sufficiently large such that

\[ -K,K\in C(F_0) \]

and

\[ F_0([-K,K])>1-\eta. \]

Since \([-K,K]\) is a continuity set of \(F_0\),

\[ F_n([-K,K]) \to F_0([-K,K]). \]

Therefore, for all sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ F_n([-K,K])>1-2\eta. \]

Hence

\[ F_0(|x|>K)<\eta \]

and eventually

\[ F_n(|x|>K)<2\eta. \]

Since the family \(\{u_t\}\) is uniformly bounded,

\[ |u_t(x)|\leq M \]

for all \(t>0\) and \(x\in\mathbb R\).

Thus, uniformly in \(t\),

\[ \begin{aligned} & \left| \int_{|x|>K}u_t(x)F_n(dx) - \int_{|x|>K}u_t(x)F_0(dx) \right|\\ &\qquad\leq \left| \int_{|x|>K}u_t(x)F_n(dx) \right| + \left| \int_{|x|>K}u_t(x)F_0(dx) \right|\\ &\qquad\leq M F_n(|x|>K) + M F_0(|x|>K)\\ &\qquad< 3M\eta. \end{aligned} \tag{1} \]

So the tail part has been controlled uniformly in \(t\).

Step 2: Partition the compact middle part

Now consider the compact interval \([-K,K]\).

Since the family \(\{u_t\}_{t>0}\) is equicontinuous, there exists \(\delta>0\), independent of \(t\), such that

\[ |x-y|<\delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |u_t(x)-u_t(y)|<\eta \]

for every \(t>0\).

The continuity set \(C(F_0)\) is dense in \(\mathbb R\), since the set of discontinuities of a distribution function is at most countable.

Therefore, we can choose a finite partition

\[ -K=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_m=K \]

such that

\[ x_j\in C(F_0), \qquad j=0,\ldots,m, \]

and

\[ x_j-x_{j-1}<\delta, \qquad j=1,\ldots,m. \]

Define

\[ I_j=(x_{j-1},x_j], \qquad j=1,\ldots,m. \]

Then

\[ [-K,K] \]

is partitioned by the finitely many intervals \(I_1,\ldots,I_m\), up to the single endpoint \(-K\), which causes no problem since \(-K\in C(F_0)\).

For each \(I_j\), choose some

\[ \xi_j\in I_j. \]

Since the diameter of \(I_j\) is less than \(\delta\), for every \(x\in I_j\),

\[ |u_t(x)-u_t(\xi_j)|<\eta \]

uniformly in \(t\).

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} & \left| \int_{I_j}u_t(x)F_n(dx) - u_t(\xi_j)F_n(I_j) \right|\\ &= \left| \int_{I_j} \left[u_t(x)-u_t(\xi_j)\right]F_n(dx) \right|\\ &\leq \eta F_n(I_j). \end{aligned} \]

Summing over \(j\),

\[ \begin{aligned} & \left| \int_{[-K,K]}u_t(x)F_n(dx) - \sum_{j=1}^m u_t(\xi_j)F_n(I_j) \right|\\ &\leq \eta\sum_{j=1}^mF_n(I_j)\\ &\leq \eta. \end{aligned} \tag{2} \]

The same argument gives

\[ \left| \int_{[-K,K]}u_t(x)F_0(dx) - \sum_{j=1}^m u_t(\xi_j)F_0(I_j) \right| \leq \eta. \tag{3} \]

Again, both bounds are independent of \(t\).

Step 3: Compare the finite approximations

We now consider

\[ \begin{aligned} & \left| \sum_{j=1}^m u_t(\xi_j)F_n(I_j) - \sum_{j=1}^m u_t(\xi_j)F_0(I_j) \right|\\ &= \left| \sum_{j=1}^m u_t(\xi_j) \left[ F_n(I_j)-F_0(I_j) \right] \right|. \end{aligned} \]

By uniform boundedness,

\[ \begin{aligned} & \left| \sum_{j=1}^m u_t(\xi_j) \left[ F_n(I_j)-F_0(I_j) \right] \right|\\ &\leq M\sum_{j=1}^m \left| F_n(I_j)-F_0(I_j) \right|. \end{aligned} \tag{4} \]

The endpoints of each \(I_j\) lie in \(C(F_0)\), so every \(I_j\) is a continuity set of \(F_0\). Hence, by weak convergence,

\[ F_n(I_j)\to F_0(I_j) \]

for every \(j=1,\ldots,m\).

There are only finitely many intervals. Therefore,

\[ \sum_{j=1}^m \left| F_n(I_j)-F_0(I_j) \right| \to0. \]

Thus, for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ M\sum_{j=1}^m \left| F_n(I_j)-F_0(I_j) \right| < \eta. \tag{5} \]

Importantly, the right-hand side of (4) does not depend on \(t\).

Step 4: Combine the three parts

Using (1)–(5), for sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ \begin{aligned} & \left| \int_{\mathbb R}u_t(x)F_n(dx) - \int_{\mathbb R}u_t(x)F_0(dx) \right|\\ &\leq \left| \int_{|x|>K}u_t(x)F_n(dx) - \int_{|x|>K}u_t(x)F_0(dx) \right|\\ &\quad+ \left| \int_{[-K,K]}u_t(x)F_n(dx) - \sum_{j=1}^m u_t(\xi_j)F_n(I_j) \right|\\ &\quad+ \left| \sum_{j=1}^m u_t(\xi_j) \left[ F_n(I_j)-F_0(I_j) \right] \right|\\ &\quad+ \left| \sum_{j=1}^m u_t(\xi_j)F_0(I_j) - \int_{[-K,K]}u_t(x)F_0(dx) \right|\\ &< 3M\eta+\eta+\eta+\eta\\ &= 3M\eta+3\eta. \end{aligned} \]

Every bound above is independent of \(t\). Therefore,

\[ \sup_{t>0} \left| \int_{\mathbb R}u_t(x)F_n(dx) - \int_{\mathbb R}u_t(x)F_0(dx) \right| < 3M\eta+3\eta \]

for all sufficiently large \(n\).

Since \(\eta>0\) is arbitrary,

\[ \boxed{ \sup_{t>0} \left| \int_{\mathbb R}u_t(x)F_n(dx) - \int_{\mathbb R}u_t(x)F_0(dx) \right| \longrightarrow0. } \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \int_{\mathbb R}u_t(x)F_n(dx) \longrightarrow \int_{\mathbb R}u_t(x)F_0(dx) } \]

uniformly in \(t\).

\(\textbf{Note:}\)

Ordinary weak convergence tells us that for each fixed bounded continuous function \(u_t\),

\[ \int u_t\,dF_n \to \int u_t\,dF_0. \]

The problem here is that \(t\) ranges over an arbitrary, possibly uncountable, family, so pointwise convergence in \(t\) is not enough.

The two assumptions on \(\{u_t\}\) play different roles.

Uniform boundedness,

\[ \sup_{t,x}|u_t(x)|\leq M, \]

allows us to make the contribution outside a large compact interval uniformly small.

Equicontinuity allows us, on that compact interval, to replace every \(u_t\) by its values at finitely many spatial points with an error that is uniform in \(t\).

Thus the proof reduces the whole uncountable family to finitely many probability masses

\[ F_n(I_1),\ldots,F_n(I_m), \]

and weak convergence handles these finitely many quantities simultaneously.

The structure is therefore

\[ \text{weak convergence} \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \text{tightness / compact truncation} \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \text{equicontinuity gives a finite spatial approximation} \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \text{weak convergence on finitely many continuity intervals} \]

\[ \Downarrow \]

\[ \text{uniform convergence in }t. \]

This is similar to the finite-grid argument in Ex 11: the main technical move is to turn an infinite uniform requirement into finitely many conditions for which one common \(N\) can be chosen.

However, in this exercise, \(G_t\) is assumed to be non-degenerate for every \(t>0\). Therefore the convergence to types argument in Step 1 applies to every \(t>0\), and hence \[ \boxed{ T=(0,\infty). } \tag{3} \] In particular, for every \(t>0\), \[ t,\ t^{-1}\in T. \]

  • Step 3: Prove that neighboring normalizations are asymptotically equivalent For fixed \(t>0\), define \[ \delta_n(t) := h_n h_{\left[ \frac{[nt]}{t} \right]}^{-1}. \] We can factor this as \[ \begin{aligned} \delta_n(t) &= h_nh_{[nt]}^{-1} \, h_{[nt]} h_{\left[ \frac{[nt]}t \right]}^{-1}\\ &= \gamma_n(t) \gamma_{[nt]}(t^{-1}). \end{aligned} \] By (3), \[ \gamma_n(t)\to\gamma(t) \] and, since \([nt]\to\infty\), \[ \gamma_{[nt]}(t^{-1}) \to \gamma(t^{-1}). \] Therefore \[ \boxed{ \delta_n(t) \longrightarrow \gamma(t)\gamma(t^{-1}). } \tag{4} \] Now take \[ t=\frac12. \] Then \[ \delta_n(1/2) = h_n h_{\left[2[n/2]\right]}^{-1}. \] If \(n=2m\) is even, \[ 2[n/2]=2m=n, \] so

\[ \delta_{2m}(1/2) = h_{2m}h_{2m}^{-1} = e. \] Thus \(e\) is a limit point of the sequence \(\{\delta_n(1/2)\}\). But by (4), the whole sequence \(\delta_n(1/2)\) already converges. Therefore its limit must equal the limit of the even subsequence, namely \(e\): \[ \boxed{ \delta_n(1/2)\to e. } \tag{5} \] Equivalently, \[ \gamma(1/2)\gamma(2)=e. \] Now suppose \(n=2m+1\) is odd. Then \[ 2[n/2]=2m=n-1, \] and therefore \[ \delta_n(1/2) = h_nh_{n-1}^{-1}. \] Using (5), \[ \boxed{ h_nh_{n-1}^{-1}\to e. } \tag{6} \] By taking finite products, for every fixed integer \(g\), \[ \boxed{ h_nh_{n+g}^{-1}\to e. } \tag{7} \] This is the key fact which allows us to ignore bounded errors created by the integer-part operation.

  • Step 4: Prove that \(\gamma\) is a homomorphism Fix \(s,t>0\). We have \[ \gamma_n(ts) = h_nh_{[nts]}^{-1}. \] Insert the intermediate normalizations \(h_{[nt]}\) and \(h_{[[nt]s]}\): \[ \begin{aligned} \gamma_n(ts) &= h_nh_{[nt]}^{-1} \, h_{[nt]}h_{[[nt]s]}^{-1} \, h_{[[nt]s]}h_{[nts]}^{-1}\\ &= \gamma_n(t) \gamma_{[nt]}(s) R_n, \end{aligned} \tag{8} \] where \[ R_n := h_{[[nt]s]}h_{[nts]}^{-1}. \] For the first two terms, \[ \gamma_n(t)\to\gamma(t) \] and, since \([nt]\to\infty\), \[ \gamma_{[nt]}(s)\to\gamma(s). \] It remains to show \[ R_n\to e. \] Since \[ [nt]\leq nt<[nt]+1, \] multiplying by \(s>0\) gives \[ [nt]s \leq nts < [nt]s+s. \] Therefore \[ 0 \leq [nts]-[[nt]s] \leq \lceil s\rceil. \] Thus the difference between the two indices is bounded by a fixed integer independent of \(n\). By (7), \[ \boxed{ R_n = h_{[[nt]s]}h_{[nts]}^{-1} \longrightarrow e. } \] Taking limits in (8), \[ \boxed{ \gamma(ts) = \gamma(t)\gamma(s). } \tag{9} \] Thus \[ \gamma:(0,\infty)\to H \] is a group homomorphism. Since \[ ts=st, \] we also have \[ \gamma(t)\gamma(s) = \gamma(s)\gamma(t). \] Thus the image of \(\gamma\) is a commutative subgroup of \(H\).

  • Step 5: Obtain the functional equations for \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) Recall \[ \gamma(t)(x) = \alpha(t)x+\beta(t). \] By (9), \[ \gamma(ts) = \gamma(t)\gamma(s). \] The right-hand side is

\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma(t)(\gamma(s)(x)) &= \alpha(t) \left[ \alpha(s)x+\beta(s) \right] + \beta(t)\\ &= \alpha(t)\alpha(s)x + \alpha(t)\beta(s) + \beta(t). \end{aligned} \] Comparing this with

\[ \gamma(ts)(x) = \alpha(ts)x+\beta(ts), \] we obtain

\[ \boxed{ \alpha(ts) = \alpha(t)\alpha(s) } \tag{10} \] and

\[ \boxed{ \beta(ts) = \alpha(t)\beta(s)+\beta(t). } \tag{11} \] Since \(ts=st\), \[ \boxed{ \beta(ts) = \alpha(s)\beta(t)+\beta(s). } \tag{12} \] These are exactly the functional equations used on p. 281.

  • Step 6: Solve the functional equations Since \(\alpha\) is positive, measurable, and satisfies \[ \alpha(ts) = \alpha(t)\alpha(s), \] the measurable Hamel equation gives \[ \boxed{ \alpha(t)=t^\theta } \] for some \(\theta\in\mathbb R\). We now consider two cases.
Case 1: \(\theta=0\)

Then \[ \alpha(t)\equiv1. \] Equation (11) becomes \[ \beta(ts) = \beta(t)+\beta(s). \] Define \[ q(x) := \beta(e^x). \] Then \[ q(x+y) = q(x)+q(y). \] Since \(q\) is measurable, the measurable Cauchy equation gives \[ q(x)=cx \] for some \(c\in\mathbb R\). Therefore \[ \boxed{ \beta(t)=c\log t. } \]

Case 2: \(\theta\neq0\)

Since \[ \alpha(t)=t^\theta, \] equations (11) and (12) give \[ \alpha(t)\beta(s)+\beta(t) = \alpha(s)\beta(t)+\beta(s). \] Thus \[ \beta(t)(1-\alpha(s)) = \beta(s)(1-\alpha(t)). \] Choose some \(s_0\neq1\). Since \(\theta\neq0\), \[ \alpha(s_0)=s_0^\theta\neq1. \] Therefore \[ \beta(t) = \frac{\beta(s_0)} {1-\alpha(s_0)} \left( 1-\alpha(t) \right). \] Writing \[ c := \frac{\beta(s_0)} {1-\alpha(s_0)}, \] we obtain \[ \boxed{ \beta(t) = c(1-t^\theta). } \] Therefore \[ \boxed{ \alpha(t)=t^\theta, } \] and \[ \boxed{ \beta(t) = \begin{cases} c\log t, & \theta=0,\\[1mm] c(1-t^\theta), & \theta\neq0. \end{cases} } \] Together with (2), \[ \boxed{ G(x) = G_t(\alpha(t)x+\beta(t)). } \] This proves the result.

\(\textbf{Note:}\) This exercise is a special case of Weissman’s result. In the original paper, Weissman does not assume that every limiting distribution \(G_t\) (or, in his notation, every \(Z_t\)) is non-degenerate. In our exercise, however, non-degeneracy of \(G_t\) is assumed for every \(t>0\). Because of this stronger assumption, convergence to types immediately gives \[ T=(0,\infty), \] whereas Weissman’s original proof needs additional facts to establish which \(t\) belong to \(T\). If we try to solve this problem only by separately tracking the scale and location terms, the error produced by \[ [[nt]s] \qquad\text{and}\qquad [nts] \] is difficult to organize. Weissman’s useful idea is to package the scale and location normalizations together into the positive affine group \(H\): \[ \gamma_n(t) = h_nh_{[nt]}^{-1}. \] Then the floor error is itself represented by a group element, \[ h_{[[nt]s]}h_{[nts]}^{-1}, \] and the statement \[ h_nh_{n+g}^{-1}\to e \] for bounded integer shifts \(g\) makes this error disappear asymptotically. Thus the abstraction is not only notation. It packages two coupled normalization errors into one object for which composition and inversion are natural. It is a fantastic experience that an exercise in the book may require us to read the original paper in order to understand the method properly. It also reminds us that we should not have the fantasy that all exercises in an advanced book must be solvable using only the knowledge explicitly developed in that book. Some exercises may come from deeper results or original papers, and part of studying them is learning when additional mathematical machinery is genuinely needed.

Ex 29

Suppose \(\{X_t,t\geq0\}\) is a family of random variables parameterized by a continuous variable \(t\) and assume there exist normalizing constants \(a(t)>0\), \(b(t)\in\mathbb R\) such that as \(t\to\infty\)

\[ \frac{X_t-b(t)}{a(t)} \Rightarrow Y, \]

where \(Y\) is non-degenerate. Show the normalizing functions can always be assumed continuous; that is, there exist continuous functions \(\alpha(t)>0\), \(\beta(t)\in\mathbb R\) such that

\[ \frac{X_t-\beta(t)}{\alpha(t)} \Rightarrow Y', \]

where \(Y'\) has a non-degenerate distribution.

(Hint: The convergence to types theorem provides normalizing constants which can be smoothed by integration.)

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

As stated, the exercise appears to be false.

Let

\[ Z\sim N(0,1), \]

and define

\[ c(t) = \begin{cases} 1, & t\in\mathbb Q,\\ 2, & t\notin\mathbb Q. \end{cases} \]

Set

\[ X_t=c(t)Z. \]

Take

\[ a(t)=c(t), \qquad b(t)=0. \]

Then, for every \(t\geq0\),

\[ \frac{X_t-b(t)}{a(t)} = \frac{c(t)Z}{c(t)} = Z. \]

Therefore

\[ \frac{X_t-b(t)}{a(t)} \Rightarrow Z \]

as \(t\to\infty\), where \(Z\) is non-degenerate. Thus all the assumptions of the exercise are satisfied.

We now show that continuous normalizing functions cannot exist.

Suppose, to the contrary, that there exist continuous functions

\[ \alpha(t)>0, \qquad \beta(t)\in\mathbb R \]

such that

\[ \frac{X_t-\beta(t)}{\alpha(t)} \Rightarrow Y' \]

for some non-degenerate random variable \(Y'\).

Choose a sequence of rational numbers

\[ r_n\to\infty. \]

Since \(r_n\in\mathbb Q\),

\[ X_{r_n}=Z, \]

and hence

\[ \frac{Z-\beta(r_n)}{\alpha(r_n)} \Rightarrow Y'. \tag{1} \]

Since \(Z\) is non-degenerate and \(Y'\) is non-degenerate, the convergence to types theorem applied to (1) implies that there exist constants

\[ A>0, \qquad B\in\mathbb R \]

such that

\[ \alpha(r_n)\to A \]

and

\[ \beta(r_n)\to B. \tag{2} \]

Moreover,

\[ Y' \overset{d}{=} \frac{Z-B}{A}. \tag{3} \]

Now use the continuity of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\).

For each \(n\), since both functions are continuous at \(r_n\), there exists \(\delta_n>0\) such that whenever

\[ |s-r_n|<\delta_n, \]

we have

\[ |\alpha(s)-\alpha(r_n)|<\frac1n \]

and

\[ |\beta(s)-\beta(r_n)|<\frac1n. \]

Because the irrational numbers are dense in \(\mathbb R\), we can choose an irrational number \(s_n\) satisfying

\[ |s_n-r_n| < \min\left(\delta_n,\frac1n\right). \]

Then

\[ s_n\to\infty, \]

and, by construction,

\[ \alpha(s_n)-\alpha(r_n)\to0, \]

and

\[ \beta(s_n)-\beta(r_n)\to0. \]

Together with (2),

\[ \alpha(s_n)\to A \]

and

\[ \beta(s_n)\to B. \tag{4} \]

However, \(s_n\) is irrational, so

\[ X_{s_n}=2Z. \]

Therefore

\[ \frac{X_{s_n}-\beta(s_n)}{\alpha(s_n)} = \frac{2Z-\beta(s_n)}{\alpha(s_n)} \Rightarrow \frac{2Z-B}{A}. \]

But by the assumed convergence along all \(t\to\infty\), the same sequence must converge to \(Y'\). Hence

\[ Y' \overset{d}{=} \frac{2Z-B}{A}. \tag{5} \]

Combining (3) and (5),

\[ \frac{Z-B}{A} \overset{d}{=} \frac{2Z-B}{A}. \]

This is impossible. Indeed,

\[ \operatorname{Var} \left( \frac{Z-B}{A} \right) = \frac1{A^2}, \]

whereas

\[ \operatorname{Var} \left( \frac{2Z-B}{A} \right) = \frac4{A^2}. \]

Since \(A>0\), the two distributions cannot be equal.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Exercise 29 is false under the assumptions as printed.} } \]

\(\textbf{Note:}\)

The hint suggests smoothing the original normalizers by integration, for example,

\[ \alpha(t) = \int_t^{t+1}a(s)\,ds, \qquad \beta(t) = \int_t^{t+1}b(s)\,ds. \]

However, under the assumptions stated in the exercise, there is no reason that these smoothed normalizers should be asymptotically equivalent to the original normalizers.

The counterexample above shows that this is not merely a missing step in the proof. Some additional regularity condition would be needed to make the conclusion true.

Rather than adding such a condition ourselves, we leave the exercise in its printed form and record the counterexample.

Ex 3

Suppose \(\{U_n,n\geq1\}\) are iid \(U(0,1)\) random variables so that

\[ P[U_j\leq x]=x,\qquad 0\leq x\leq1. \]

  1. Show

\[ \prod_{j=1}^n U_j^{1/n} \]

converges almost surely. What is the limit?

  1. Center and scale the sequence

\[ \left\{ \prod_{j=1}^n U_j^{1/n},\ n\geq1 \right\} \]

and show the resulting sequence converges in distribution to a non-degenerate limit. To which one?

\(\textbf{Answer:}\)

(a)

Take logarithms:

\[ \log\left( \prod_{j=1}^n U_j^{1/n} \right) = \frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\log U_j. \]

Since \(\{U_j\}\) are iid, so are \(\{\log U_j\}\).

Also,

\[ E|\log U_1| = \int_0^1 |\log x|\,dx < \infty. \]

Therefore, by the SLLN,

\[ \frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\log U_j \overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow} E(\log U_1). \]

Now

\[ E(\log U_1) = \int_0^1\log x\,dx. \]

Using integration by parts,

\[ \int \log x\,dx = x\log x-x, \]

so

\[ E(\log U_1) = \left[x\log x-x\right]_0^1 = -1. \]

Hence

\[ \log\left( \prod_{j=1}^n U_j^{1/n} \right) \overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow} -1. \]

Since the exponential function is continuous,

\[ \boxed{ \prod_{j=1}^n U_j^{1/n} \overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow} e^{-1}. } \]

(b)

Let

\[ L_n = \frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\log U_j. \]

From part (a),

\[ E(\log U_1)=-1. \]

We also have

\[ E\left[(\log U_1)^2\right] = \int_0^1(\log x)^2\,dx. \]

Using integration by parts,

\[ \int (\log x)^2\,dx = x(\log x)^2 - 2x\log x + 2x, \]

and therefore

\[ E\left[(\log U_1)^2\right] = 2. \]

Thus

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\log U_1) = 2-(-1)^2 = 1. \]

By the CLT,

\[ \frac{ \sqrt n\left(L_n-(-1)\right) }{ \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\log U_1)} } \Rightarrow N(0,1). \]

Since \(\operatorname{Var}(\log U_1)=1\),

\[ \boxed{ \sqrt n(L_n+1) \Rightarrow N(0,1). } \tag{1} \]

Now define

\[ g(x)=e^x. \]

Then

\[ \prod_{j=1}^n U_j^{1/n} = e^{L_n} = g(L_n), \]

and

\[ g(-1)=e^{-1}, \qquad g'(-1)=e^{-1}. \]

Applying the delta method to (1),

\[ \sqrt n \left( e^{L_n}-e^{-1} \right) \Rightarrow e^{-1}N(0,1). \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{ \displaystyle \prod_{j=1}^n U_j^{1/n}-e^{-1} }{ e^{-1}/\sqrt n } \Rightarrow N(0,1). } \]

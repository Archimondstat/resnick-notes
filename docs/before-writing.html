Notes on A Probability Path (Resnick)

Before Writing

Ask yourself these questions.

1. What problem does this section solve?

Do not start with the theorem.

Start with the motivation.

Examples:

  • Heavy-tailed random variables have no finite moments.
  • We need to study partial sums.
  • We need a criterion for almost sure convergence.

If I cannot answer this question, I probably only know the theorems, not the section.

2. What new tool did this section introduce?

Every section usually contributes one important idea.

Examples:

  • truncation
  • tail equivalence
  • maximal inequality
  • Borel–Cantelli
  • stopping times

Do not list every proposition.

Find the new weapon.

3. Why was this tool needed?

Instead of writing

Proposition 7.x.x

answer

What difficulty does this tool overcome?

The theorem statement can always be looked up later.

The motivation cannot.

4. What path did the section follow?

Describe the development instead of listing theorem numbers.

Example:

Heavy tails

↓

Truncation

↓

Tail equivalence

↓

Ordinary WLLN

↓

Generalized WLLN

or

Need to study partial sums

↓

Skorohod inequality

↓

Lévy theorem

↓

Kolmogorov convergence criterion

The arrows are often more valuable than the theorem names.

5. What is the main proof idea?

Do not summarize every proof.

Instead answer:

What was the key idea?

Usually one sentence is enough.

Examples:

  • truncate first
  • compare with an easier sequence
  • bound the maximal partial sum
  • apply Borel–Cantelli
  • reduce to the previous theorem

6. What new capability do I have now?

Finish every summary by answering:

After learning this section, what can I now do that I couldn’t do before?

Examples:

  • I now know how to handle heavy-tailed random variables.
  • I now know how to transfer convergence through tail equivalence.
  • I now know how to investigate convergence of partial sums.
  • I now know when convergence in probability can be upgraded to almost sure convergence.

A good section should leave me with a new capability, not just another theorem.

Writing Principles

  • Use my own language.
  • Do not imitate the textbook.
  • Focus on understanding rather than reproduction.
  • Capture the mathematical journey.
  • Record the architecture instead of every brick.

If my future self can rebuild the section from this summary, then the summary has succeeded.

Evaluation Rubric

Each summary will be evaluated according to the following criteria.

1. Motivation (20%)

Did I explain why this section exists?

Questions:

  • What problem is being solved?
  • Why do we need this section?

2. New Tool (20%)

Did I identify the main mathematical idea introduced?

Questions:

  • What new weapon did I obtain?
  • Why is it useful?

3. Path / Architecture (25%)

Did I explain how the section develops?

Questions:

  • Does one result naturally lead to the next?
  • Can I see the overall flow?

4. Mathematical Accuracy (20%)

Is my description mathematically correct?

Small imprecisions are acceptable.

Conceptual mistakes are not.

5. Personal Reconstruction (15%)

Does this sound like my understanding instead of a compressed copy of the book?

Could my future self rebuild the section from these notes?

Overall Goal

A successful summary is not one that reproduces Resnick.

It is one that allows my future self to navigate the mathematical landscape again.

The book provides the path.

The map is mine.

Note on Chapters 1–3: I have already worked through the exercises for Chapters 1–3 during my initial study, so I will randomly or quasi-randomly select exercises from Chapters 1-3 to refill the blank, and may or may not write down notes for these chapters.

  • Some idea summary, sometimes the proof in the book may be too long so I write down something to summarize the idea.
